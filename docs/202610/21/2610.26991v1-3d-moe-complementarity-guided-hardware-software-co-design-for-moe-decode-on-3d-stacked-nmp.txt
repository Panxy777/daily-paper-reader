Abstract —Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) have become a main-stream way to scale Large Language Models (LLMs), because each token activates only a small subset of experts, reducing inference cost. However, MoE decode remains highly memory-bound and exhibits strongly skewed, time-varying expert loads. 3D-stacked DRAM near-memory processing (NMP) provides the bandwidth that decode demands, yet its tiled organization makes load balancing expensive: expert parallelism (EP) preserves locality but suffers from hotspot-induced imbalance, while more distributed strategies weaken locality and increase NoC traffic. To address this locality-balance conflict, we observe that domain-specialized token routing creates offsetting expert-load fluctuations across decode steps, a property we call expert complementarity. Using this insight, we propose 3D-MoE, ahardware–software co-designed system for MoE decode on 3D-stacked NMP. 3D-MoE uses offline, one-time complementarity-guided grouping and locality-aware mapping to establish coarse-grained inter-group balance, handles residual intra-group hotspots through a lightweight Collaborative Memory-Network (CMN) that streams each hot expert’s weight tiles once from its host DRAM channel to helper cores over a reconfigured local broadcast path, and uses cost-aware online remapping to remain robust to workload drift. Across MoE models, 3D-MoE achieves an average 4.63 × MoE-layer speedup over EP and improves end-to-end throughput by 1.58 ×–1.86 × over the strongest NMP baselines, with 2.11% additional logic-die area. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have become main-stream for scaling Large Language Models (LLMs): each token activates only a small expert subset, decoupling model capacity from inference cost [10], [30]. Yet this sparsity makes autoregressive decoding highly memory-bound; each expert receives few tokens per step, producing small GEMMs with poor data reuse [88]. In our profiling, MoE layers account for 83–92% of end-to-end latency at batch size 64 and dominate serving latency [25]. Expert loads are also highly skewed and dynamic, with the hottest expert receiving over 20 × the step-average token count [13]. 3D-stacked DRAM near-memory processing (NMP) is well matched to bandwidth-dominated MoE decode by serving expert weights directly from local stacked DRAM [14], [68], [80]. Yet its tiled organization creates a severe cost asymmetry: local accesses are fast, whereas remote accesses traverse the network-on-chip (NoC) and consume shared bandwidth. MoE deployment consequently faces a three-way tension among locality, load balance, and communication cost. Expert Par-allelism (EP) best matches this structure by keeping expert weights local, but suffers from routing skew. Tensor Paral-lelism (TP) smooths load, yet turns every expert invocation into a distributed operation with synchronization and NoC overhead. Dynamic EP methods partially alleviate hotspots, but the difficulty of predicting expert loads and handling remote hotspots limits their benefits on NMP [8], [13]. The central challenge is therefore to retain EP’s locality while mitigating its imbalance. 

Our key observation is that MoE routing skew is not purely random. Although MoE training often includes load-balancing objectives, pre-trained models develop structured expert preferences, often reflected in domain-skewed activation patterns [56], [75]. As the request mix changes, demand shifts across expert subsets and induces offsetting variations in their decode-step loads. We call this trace-level load cancellation 

expert complementarity . This structural signal suggests an EP-friendly balancing mechanism: grouping complementary experts and mapping them to nearby NMP cores can stabilize aggregate group load without sacrificing weight locality. Turning complementarity into a practical deployment prim-itive remains non-trivial. First, highly volatile step-level token routing obscures the useful complementarity signal, requiring a robust method to distinguish persistent relations from transient noise. Second, complementarity improves balance mainly at the group level , leaving transient hotspots within a group. Because a hot expert’s weights remain pinned to its host core, nearby helper cores require repeated remote weight fetches or inefficient packet-routed one-to-many delivery. Third, the offline expert deployment derived from historical traces must remain robust under workload drift, calling for lightweight 1arXiv:2610.26991v1 [cs.AR] 19 Oct 2026 MoE Layer

To address these challenges, we propose 3D-MoE, ahardware–software co-design for MoE decode on 3D-stacked NMP. 3D-MoE exploits complementarity hierarchically. First, it profiles decode-step expert loads on representative mixed workloads to filter transient routing noise and expose persistent complementary structure in the traces. It then performs one-time complementarity-guided expert grouping and mapping through a two-stage offline optimization that accounts for inter-group balance, expected residual collaboration cost, and communication-aware placement. Second, we introduce the 

Collaborative Memory-Network (CMN) , a lightweight co-design of the memory controller and NoC that handles residual intra-group hotspots through a specialized “load once, broad-cast to many” path: the host reads each hot expert’s weight tile once from local DRAM and streams it to helper cores over a temporary broadcast path created by lightweight NoC reconfiguration. • We identify expert complementarity as a trace-level load-cancellation structure in MoE decode, and validate that it is sufficiently stable across datasets and time windows to support one-time offline deployment. 

• We formulate complementarity-guided expert grouping and locality-aware mapping as sequential optimization stages that balance inter-group load while accounting for intra-group collaboration and routing costs. 

• We design CMN, a lightweight hardware fast path for residual intra-group hotspot assistance that reduces re-dundant DRAM reads and per-hop packet processing.

II. B ACKGROUND AND DESIGN IMPLICATIONS 

A. MoE Architecture & Decode Behavior 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) has become a pivotal architecture for scaling Large Language Models (LLMs) efficiently [9], [10], [12], [30], [75]. As shown in Fig. 1(a), it replaces each Transformer Feed-Forward Network (FFN) with parallel experts. A lightweight gate selects only a few experts for each token, scaling model capacity while keeping per-token computation bounded [3], [10]. Training-time load-balancing objectives do not eliminate expert specialization: pre-trained models often develop domain-preferred experts [45], [75], so different request domains activate different expert subsets rather than all experts uniformly (Fig. 2(a)). Efficient MoE inference remains challenging, especially during autoregressive decode. Even under batching, each ex-pert receives few tokens per step, fragmenting execution into small GEMMs or GEMVs with poor data reuse and low arithmetic intensity [25]. MoE layers consequently account for 83 –92% of end-to-end latency at batch size 64 in our profiling, consistent with prior studies [66], as shown in Fig. 1(b). Meanwhile, expert loads are highly skewed and rapidly changing [13]. As shown in Fig. 2, only a few experts become hot at a step; the hottest expert can receive over 20 ×

the step-average load, while each expert’s own load varies by 

2–7× across steps and layers. MoE decode therefore poses two coupled challenges: it is highly memory-bound, yet its expert loads are also severely imbalanced and time-varying.

The memory-bound nature of MoE decode is well matched to 3D-stacked Near-Memory Processing (NMP), whereas con-ventional GPUs/NPUs often hit the memory wall [55], [56]. In contrast, 3D-stacked NMP [1], [14], [35], [53], [63], [68], [73], [80], [83], [89] leverages the existing base logic die beneath the stacked DRAM dies for near-memory compute. Fabricated in an advanced logic process, this die can accommodate substantially more compute en-gines than in-DRAM designs, while dense through-silicon vias (TSVs) provide high-bandwidth, low-latency data access. This bandwidth–compute balance matches MoE decode (Fig. 1(c)); Fig. 3 shows the HBM-style instance evaluated here. Recent systems such as Duplex and Stratum [56], [80] adopt a heterogeneous xPU–NMP organization: the xPU ex-ecutes compute-intensive operators, while NMP logic han-dles bandwidth-bound operators near DRAM. In our HBM-style instance, each logic-die core is tightly coupled to an HBM channel, forming a compute–memory unit; these cores communicate through a NoC. We call this unit an NMP core. Each core integrates a GEMM engine for matrix and nonlinear operations, on-die buffers, a channel controller, and a NoC router [80]. This tiled organization provides abundant parallelism and massive aggregate bandwidth, enabling experts to be distributed across cores and executed near data. However, realizing these benefits requires expert execution to remain near its designated memory partition. 

C. Parallelizing MoE on Distributed NMP Cores 

Parallelizing MoE on this tiled architecture is difficult because NMP has a strong cost asymmetry : local DRAM accesses are fast and bandwidth-rich, whereas remote accesses traverse the NoC and consume shared bandwidth. An effective design must therefore preserve locality, balance load, and control communication cost, as summarized in Fig. 4. 

Expert Parallelism (EP) maps each expert to dedicated NMP cores [41]. EP preserves expert locality, forms larger expert GEMMs by aggregating routed tokens [10], and keeps each token’s routing fan-out independent of total expert count under fixed top-k [33]. However, skewed and dynamic expert loads (Fig. 2) turn cores hosting hot experts into stragglers. 

Tensor Parallelism (TP) distributes each expert across cores and smooths its work [62]. On 3D-stacked NMP, however, every invocation becomes a distributed execution followed by reduction-style synchronization over the mesh NoC [20], [27]; in our analysis, NoC overhead reaches 52% of expert execution time at 32 cores. Weight partitioning also weakens local reuse within each core’s GEMM engine. Thus, TP sacrifices the locality that benefits memory-bound decode. Restricting TP to nearby cores or hot experts in hybrid schemes [26] reduces, but does not remove, the partitioning and synchronization costs. Pipeline Parallelism (PP) is not considered, since splitting decode batches into micro-batches weakens data reuse and introduces pipeline bubbles [28]. 

Dynamic optimization on top of EP. Given TP’s com-munication overhead, many systems retain EP and repair its imbalance through expert remapping, replication, or task stealing [2], [8], [15], [21], [48], [61], [64], [69], [71], [77], [81], [82]. These methods are less effective on NMP for two reasons. First, step- and layer-level variation makes hot-expert prediction difficult and limits the effectiveness of proactive reallocation; prior work reports below 50% accuracy [13]. Second, weight migration and remote helper requests consume the NoC bandwidth on which NMP locality depends. Overall, EP is the most suitable backbone for MoE on 3D-stacked NMP : it best matches the platform’s locality-oriented cost structure. The remaining challenge is to balance load without sacrificing locality. 

D. Design Implications for EP Deployment 

Modern MoE models may contain 128 or more experts, while NMP exposes a fine-grained multi-core architecture, making global core-level load balance difficult. A more natural objective is to avoid localized hotspots: partition experts into groups whose aggregate loads remain balanced over time, rather than allowing multiple hot experts to cluster in the same physical region. Residual imbalance is then confined within each group, where its impact and the cost of further optimization are lower. III. E XPERT COMPLEMENTARITY 

A. Domain-Specialized Routing and Expert Complementarity 

As in Sec. II-A, MoE routing uses a learned gating network. Although MoE training includes load-balancing objectives, routed experts still specialize by domain rather than serving all inputs uniformly [11], [59]. This tendency strengthens in many-expert models: as shared experts absorb common patterns, routed experts become domain-specialized [6], [75]. Figure 2(a) shows that different Qwen-MoE domains prefer different expert subsets; our profiling further finds that over 90% of hits in math- and code-related workloads fall within the corresponding preferred subsets, consistent with Stratum [56]. When the request mix changes, demand shifts across these subsets, causing some expert loads to rise while others fall. We call this trace-level load cancellation expert complementar-ity . Because this structure reflects learned specialization rather than random fluctuations, grouping complementary experts can stabilize aggregate group load as targeted in Sec. II-D. 

B. Trace Construction and Metric Definition 

We operationalize complementarity with decode-step-level expert-load traces . For each layer, expert e is represented by its routed-token count Fe,d at step d under batched serving. Figure 5 shows two examples. Highly complementary traces exhibit anti-phase behavior and yield a smoother aggregate load, whereas low-complementarity traces co-vary or show no systematic cancellation. We quantify load offsetting with Pearson correlation be-tween expert traces: experts that consistently offset each other’s token volume can smooth aggregate runtime load when grouped. Instead of using a model-independent threshold, we construct an empirical null distribution for each model– dataset setting by repeatedly circularly shifting one expert trace relative to the other and recomputing the correlation [5]. This preserves each trace’s marginal load while disrupting temporal alignment [79]. A pair is significantly complemen-tary when its observed correlation falls below the lower α-quantile of this null (the 5th percentile for α = 0 .05 ). C. Validation and Stability of Complementarity 

We first test whether complementarity is consistent with domain-specialized routing. For each expert, we build a nor-malized preference profile from four representative datasets: HumanEval [87] for code, MATH [23] for math, SciQ [72] for science, and LogiQA [47] for logic. Table I shows that complementary pairs have lower cosine similarity than posi-tively correlated and random pairs across models, supporting our interpretation that complementarity is associated with load shifts across domain-preferred expert subsets. We then evaluate three MoE models across three serving datasets. For each model–dataset setting, Table II reports the average number of significant complementary partners per expert, the fraction of experts with at least one partner, and partner retention on the other datasets for the same model. Complementarity is prevalent: 82.2%–88.3% of experts have at least one partner, with 9.6 partners per expert on average. Moreover, 70.6%–80.5% of partners identified on one dataset remain significant in the other datasets, indicating substantial 

cross-dataset stability .We also examine temporal stability using serving traces. We identify significant complementary partners from the first 256 decode steps and track their retention over the next three 256-step windows. Retention is 94.5%, 92.7%, and 90.8%, respectively. Thus, although individual experts exhibit volatile step-level loads, the complementary structure remains stable over time. 

D. From Complementarity to Group-Level Load Balance 

We next test whether complementarity improves load bal-ance across expert groups. Figure 6 compares three MoE 4models on LMSys under matched group sizes. We quantify per-step imbalance using the max-to-mean load ratio (M2M) ,i.e., the maximum group load divided by the mean group load; lower is better and 1 is ideal. Unlike coarse history-load-guided expert grouping [15], [46], our method groups experts using complementarity from finer-grained decode traces, as detailed in Sec. IV-B. Both methods use historical traces for grouping and later traces for evaluation. Across the three models, our method reduces average M2M by 17%, keeping it near 1.1, demonstrating effective inter-group load balancing. A more comprehensive evaluation appears in Sec. V-B. The benefit is primarily group-level: complementary experts smooth aggregate load, but individual experts within a group may still exhibit transient imbalance. Under mixed-domain serving, preferred subsets can offset one another; under ho-mogeneous serving, experts with similar preferences are less likely to cluster within the same group. Residual intra-group hotspots are handled by the lightweight hardware support in Sec. IV-D. Both expert grouping and mapping rely only on decode-step load statistics, not explicit domain labels. IV. 3D-M OE S YSTEM 

A. System Overview 

Building on the complementarity in Sec. III, we propose 3D-MoE, a hardware–software co-designed system for high-throughput MoE inference on 3D-stacked NMP. 3D-MoE combines offline expert grouping and mapping, lightweight hardware support, and online scheduling and remapping. 

(1) Offline Grouping & Mapping. Using decode-step load traces, complementarity-guided expert grouping balances ag-gregate loads across expert groups, while locality-aware map-ping places each group’s experts on nearby NMP cores to localize collaboration. 

(2) Hardware Support. Complementarity improves group-level balance but leaves transient intra-group hotspots. We therefore propose the Collaborative Memory-Network (CMN), which reads each hot expert’s weight tile once from its host core’s local DRAM and streams it to nearby helper cores over a reconfigured broadcast path, avoiding repeated remote reads and per-hop processing in the packet-switched NoC. 

(3) Online Scheduling & Remapping. At runtime, intra-group scheduling selects helper cores and configures CMN for step-level hotspots, while cost-aware remapping adjusts expert replicas and placement under longer-term workload drift. 

Workflow and Scope. 3D-MoE targets MoE layers during autoregressive decode, which account for 83–92% of end-to-end latency. Following the cooperative xPU–NMP model [80], the xPU handles prefill and compute-intensive dense operators, while NMP executes bandwidth-bound attention, gating, and MoE experts. Routing metadata then drives token dispatch, expert execution, collaboration, and output gathering on NMP. For dense models, 3D-MoE bypasses the expert-specific mech-anisms and follows the baseline path [43], [80].

We therefore select group size according to model and hardware constraints and keep each group within one stack whenever capacity permits. C. Hardware Motivation for Residual Intra-Group Hotspots 

Complementarity stabilizes aggregate group load but leaves transient imbalance among experts within a group. These localized intra-group hotspots may still require nearby under-loaded cores to assist a hot expert. On 3D-stacked NMP, such collaboration is costly because the hot expert’s weights remain attached to the host core. There are three representative ways to handle such hotspots on NMP. In on-demand remote fetch , every helper requests weight tiles from the host, repeating host service, increasing memory contention, and stalling helpers on NoC transfers. 

Single-read + NoC broadcast eliminates redundant DRAM reads, but its one-to-many stream still traverses the general packet-switched network. Intra-group TP distributes an expert across nearby cores, but every invocation requires partitioned execution and reduction, while weight partitioning weakens local reuse. Therefore, effective hotspot assistance should preserve EP residency and reduce the overhead of assistance. We build a hotspot-expert benchmark on NMP to quantify these alternatives for 2–16 cores collaborating on one hot expert. Table IV shows that remote fetch and intra-group TP exceed the ideal latency by 29–242% and 19–185%, respec-tively; single-read NoC broadcast still adds 48% on average. Table V explains this gap: fixed router stages contribute 63.2% of average flit latency, versus only 7.8% from dynamic wait-ing. CMN/RCL avoids repeated per-hop processing through reserved-link streaming during expert computation. These results motivate a single-read, multi-helper path that avoids repeated host service and packet-switched buffering, routing, and VC allocation [36], [37]. Such a path directly matches the structured one-to-many weight stream, which conventional multihop NoCs handle inefficiently [51].

Fig. 8. CMN comprises a Data Forwarding Unit (DFU), a Data Receiving Unit (DRU), and a Reconfigurable Collaboration Link (RCL) in each NMP core. During collaboration, when the host core fetches a hot expert’s weight tile from its local 3D-stacked memory for computation, CMN simultaneously streams the same tile to helper cores over a temporary, bus-like local broadcast path. Thus, a single DRAM read serves all participating cores without repeated remote requests or replicated packet delivery over the regular NoC. 

Data forwarding and reception. The DFU and DRU implement this single-read, multi-helper path (Fig. 8(b)). The DFU is integrated into the 3D memory controller after the Read Return Buffer. Before each collaboration task, the controller configures the DFU with the hot expert’s address range and helper-core mask. When a matching DRAM read returns, the DFU forwards the tile to both the host core’s local Weight Buffer and the configured RCL path. Each helper’s DRU captures the incoming stream and writes it to its local Weight Buffer for immediate GEMM consumption. Helper cores therefore consume the hot expert’s weights as a streamed local input rather than issuing individual remote requests. 

Reconfigurable collaboration link. To distribute streamed weight tiles efficiently, the RCL reuses existing NoC links in two phase-aligned modes: standard packet transport for token dispatch/gather and temporary local broadcast for hot-expert collaboration [52], [65], [70]. During decode, token dispatch and output gathering use packet transport, whereas residual-hotspot collaboration streams the same weight tiles from one host to several nearby helpers. Because these patterns occur in different phases, the same physical links can support both modes without concurrent contention. Each router integrates a lightweight Bus Switch and a distributed Session Arbiter. The Bus Switch detaches selected links from the packet pipeline and reconnects them into a tem-porary bus-like broadcast path [84], while the Session Arbiter grants exclusive access among collaborating hosts. Compared with replicated packets sent through the regular router pipeline, this path avoids repeated per-hop packet processing during expert computation. E. Intra-group Expert Scheduling 

CMN enables low-cost collaboration, but realizing its bene-fits requires scheduling to identify hotspot experts per decode step and form collaboration domains and schedules. Because gating exposes per-expert loads before expert execution, the stack controller can use this window to run the latency-driven heuristic in Algorithm 1. The heuristic selects nearby helpers and constructs benefit-guided, link-disjoint collaboration do-mains and schedules to reduce group latency. As Fig. 7(b) shows, expert metadata converts per-expert token counts into estimated core latencies through a pre-measured performance table [34], [74]. Based on this latency profile, the scheduler starts from the most overloaded source core and incrementally expands its broadcast domain with underloaded helpers, forming candidate collaboration domains Topology new . This allows part of the hot expert’s routed tokens to be reassigned to the newly added helper, while CMN broadcasts the expert’s weights from the source. Once the current hotspot is relieved, the scheduler proceeds to the next most overloaded core. Each candidate is limited to at most 16 cores within one stack and must be link-disjoint from active domains. The scheduler greedily retains beneficial candidates after accounting for CMN collaboration cost and generates the corresponding ordered collaboration schedule. This procedure runs on the stack controller with compact metadata. Each expert group spans only 8–16 cores in our settings, keeping metadata small. A compact performance table covering up to 2048 tokens per expert supports the evaluated batched-decode settings with about 4 KB of SRAM. 

F. Online Expert Remapping 

Although offline grouping and mapping provide a strong initial layout, MoE workload drift can create imbalances and reduce performance by up to 15% in our evaluation. 3D-MoE therefore uses cost-aware online expert remapping that combines frequent low-cost adjustments with selective higher-cost reconfigurations. Online remapping uses metadata maintained by the stack controller to predict expert loads. The metadata records expert-to-core mappings, replica locations, and per-step workloads. For each expert prediction, we maintain an exponential moving average (EMA), EMA e(t) = αF e(t) + (1 − α)EMA e(t − 1) ,where Fe(t) is expert e’s load at step t. The smoothing parameter α weights the latest observation against historical trends; we set α = 0 .3 based on profiling-trace prediction accuracy. EMA remains responsive to recent changes while suppressing noise, helping identify sustained load drift. 

Multi-Tiered Adjustment. Based on migration cost, we use three adjustment tiers (Fig. 7(c)). Tier-1 redistributes load fractions among existing expert replicas without moving weights. Tier-2 adds a replica for an overloaded expert and removes a redundant replica from an underutilized one; the new replica overwrites the freed space and requires one weight transfer. Tier-3 swaps experts across groups to correct severe inter-group imbalance, requiring two weight migrations. 

Cost-Aware Trigger. Migration-free Tier-1 runs before every MoE layer. Tier-2 and Tier-3 candidates are checked every eight decode steps or earlier when sustained imbalance is detected. The eight-step interval is selected from profiling traces. Sustained imbalance is detected when EMA-predicted group-level M2M exceeds the profiled steady-state level by 20% for three consecutive decode steps. At each check, the controller estimates candidate benefits from EMA-predicted loads, subtracts migration costs based on moved expert-weight volume, transfer distance, and available bandwidth, and exe-cutes the highest-benefit action only if net benefit is positive. This paper presents 3D-MoE, a hardware–software co-designed system for MoE decode on 3D-stacked NMP.
