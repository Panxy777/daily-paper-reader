Abstract 

Training language models whose parameters and optimizer state exceed a single node’s memory requires sharding these tensors across devices. Under tensor parallelism (TP) or fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP), matrix-aware optimizers such as Muon intro-duce communication to perform global matrix operations across shards. We propose Dobb (Distributed Optimizer with Block-diagonal Eigen bases), a matrix-aware optimizer de-signed to compute updates locally within parameter shards. Dobb approximates the input-activation second moment with diagonal blocks and tracks a complete basis within each block. It projects momentum into these bases, applies sign normalization to the coeffi-cients, and reconstructs the update. When each block lies within one shard under TP or FSDP, Dobb applies its matrix update locally, requiring no communication beyond data-parallel synchronization of the activation second moments. We benchmark Dobb against Dion, a distributed matrix-aware optimizer designed to retain the benefits of orthonormal-ized updates while reducing communication. On GPT-2 XL (1.56B parameters), Dobb with 16 blocks reaches a validation loss of 2.476, compared with 2.511 for Dion at rank fraction 1/16. Under FSDP on 32 H100 GPUs, Dobb reduces the amortized optimizer-call time from 124.1 ms for Dion to 9.2 ms. 02.46 2.50 2.54 2.58

1arXiv:2610.39412v1 [cs.LG] 29 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Language-model scaling increases the memory requirements for parameters, gradients, and opti-mizer state (Kaplan et al., 2020; Rajbhandari et al., 2020). When these quantities exceed a single GPU’s capacity, training distributes them across devices through tensor parallelism (TP) or fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP) (Shoeybi et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023). Under either layout, each device holds one part of every sharded weight matrix, and we call this part a shard; data-parallel replicas hold copies of the same shards. Cross-shard communication is communication among the devices that hold different shards of one matrix. These layouts make matrix-aware optimization a distributed problem: an update defined on a complete weight matrix may require information held by several devices. Muon orthogonalizes momentum (Jordan et al., 2024), which introduces dense optimizer-specific communication in conventional sharded implementations (Ahn et al., 2025; Khaled et al., 2026). MuonBP reduces communication by performing local Newton–Schulz orthogonalization between periodic global steps, but those global steps still require full cross-shard communication (Khaled et al., 2026). The frequency of global orthogonalization controls the tradeoff between communica-tion cost and optimization quality. Another approach, Dion, reduces the size of each exchange by approximating momentum with low-rank factors and retaining the residual through error feedback (Ahn et al., 2025). Its optimizer step communicates these factors across shards, so communication scales with the update rank. In this work, we introduce Dobb ( Distributed Optimizer with Block-diagonal Eigen bases) to reduce optimizer communication. We approximate the activation second moment by a block-diagonal ma-trix and retain a complete eigenbasis within every block. This construction discards cross-block correlations without imposing a reduced-rank constraint on the update. When these blocks align with shard boundaries, the optimizer step requires no cross-shard communication beyond the data-parallel synchronization of the activation second moments. Dion derives its low-rank factors from momentum; we instead project momentum onto an input-side basis from an exponentially averaged activation second moment, normalize the coefficients, and reconstruct the update. For a mini-batch gradient, projection into its own activation eigenbasis separates input spectral scales from the output-gradient structure, leaving the latter in the coefficients (Section 3). In our GPT-2 Small experiments, activation second moments exhibit greater spectral concentration than output-gradient second moments in most matched matrices (Figure 2). This motivates using the activation eigenbasis to expose the more concentrated factor’s directional scaling for normalization (Appendix F.2). Both sign and column normalization remove positive input spectral scales from a mini-batch gradi-ent expressed in its own activation eigenbasis. We choose the element-wise sign, which addition-ally removes magnitude differences within each coefficient column. In our synthetic comparison, this choice produces more orthogonal updates than column normalization except when the output-gradient spectrum is nearly flat (Table 1). Sign normalization also avoids the norm reduction re-quired by column normalization when the output axis is sharded, because it acts on each coefficient independently. We make four contributions: • We introduce Dobb, which normalizes momentum coefficients in block-diagonal activation second-moment eigenbases while retaining every direction within each block. • Dobb requires only that each block lie within one shard. Under this condition, the op-timizer step introduces zero optimizer-specific cross-shard bytes beyond the data-parallel synchronization of the activation second moments. • We compare Dobb with Muon and Dion on GPT-2-style models up to 1.56B parameters under a shared update-scale protocol. Across these scales, Dobb exhibits less validation-loss degradation under block partitioning than Dion under corresponding rank reductions. • We benchmark Dobb against Muon and Dion under FSDP and tensor parallelism on GPT-2-style models up to 8.1B parameters and on Qwen3 models up to 32B. In every measured configuration, Dobb’s optimizer call is shorter than those of Muon and Dion (Section 6.2). 22 Related work 

Matrix-aware optimization. Matrix-aware optimizers use the intrinsic two-dimensional struc-ture of weight matrices to construct parameter updates rather than treating each entry independently. Muon orthogonalizes momentum using Newton–Schulz iterations, while RMNP uses row normal-ization (Jordan et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2026). K-FAC builds a Kronecker-factored approximation to layerwise curvature (Martens and Grosse, 2015). Shampoo accumulates gradient-derived matrix second moments, and SOAP applies Adam in their eigenbases (Gupta et al., 2018; Vyas et al., 2025). 

Sharding and tensor parallelism. ZeRO reduces memory redundancy across data-parallel work-ers by progressively sharding optimizer state, gradients, and parameters across its three stages (Ra-jbhandari et al., 2020). FSDP shards all three and gathers parameters as needed for forward and backward computation (Zhao et al., 2023). FSDP2 uses per-parameter sharding, which facilitates composition with tensor parallelism (Liang et al., 2025). Tensor parallelism, as used in Megatron-LM, partitions weight matrices and their matrix multiplications across devices (Shoeybi et al., 2019). The resulting parameter layout determines which operands are local and which require communica-tion during the optimizer step. 

Distributed matrix updates. While element-wise optimizer updates parallelize naturally, matrix-aware updates introduce dependencies among optimizer state, matrix computations, and the under-lying parameter layout. Dion retains a full-size momentum buffer distributed across shards and reduces communication through low-rank factor operations and error feedback, with communica-tion scaling with the update rank (Ahn et al., 2025). Distributed Shampoo takes a different approach. It distributes preconditioner state and computation by parameter block within data-parallel process groups, then all-gathers the computed search directions within each group at every optimizer step (Shi et al., 2023). MatrixFSDP takes a systems approach under FSDP: it places each whole ma-trix on one owner rank, so Muon runs unchanged without optimizer-step collectives, and it leaves matrices sharded by tensor parallelism outside its scope (Gao et al., 2026).

Spectral concentration motivates the input-side basis. In transformer training, weight gradients (Shen et al., 2026) and updates from SGD with momentum and Adam (Jordan et al., 2024) typically have low-rank structure. Muon approximately orthogonalizes its updates, making their singular values nearly equal, and achieves model quality comparable to AdamW with less compute (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). We compare activation and output-gradient second moments using entropy-based effective rank, normalized by the factor dimension, across layer types and training checkpoints (Figure 2). A lower effective rank indicates that the spectrum is concentrated in fewer eigendirections. Figure 2 shows that activation factors exhibit greater spectral concentration than the corresponding output-gradient factors. At 50% of training, over the 72 weight matrices of GPT-2 Small with the query, key, and value projections counted separately, the median effective rank is 13% for the activation second moment and 55% for the output-gradient second moment, and the output-gradient factor has the higher effective rank in 65 of the 72 pairs. Shen et al. (2026) report highly concentrated singular values in the input feature matrices of MNIST and CIFAR-10 and in RoBERTa text embeddings. An eigenbasis exposes spectral concentration as unequal scales along its coordinate directions. This motivates using the activation eigenbasis to remove the more concentrated factor’s directional scaling through sign normalization. Temporal stability motivates basis reuse. K-FAC exploits slowly changing second-moment fac-tors and relatively stable curvature to amortize inverse computations across optimizer steps (Martens and Grosse, 2015, Appendix G). Following this rationale, we reuse the activation-side basis between periodic refreshes, reducing the amortized cost of basis computation. Appendix F.4 evaluates the re-sulting tradeoff between iteration time and optimization quality. 

Sharding motivates a block-diagonal basis. Under TP and FSDP alike, each rank holds one shard of a weight matrix and the matching shards of its gradient and optimizer state, so a matrix-aware update of the complete matrix needs operands from other shards. We therefore seek a basis that each rank can construct and apply from its own operands: a block-diagonal basis whose blocks align with shard boundaries. We write TP for the tensor-parallel degree, FS for the FSDP shard degree, and B for the number of second-moment blocks. In the Megatron-LM layout, a column-parallel layer shards the output axis m and receives a replicated input, whereas a row-parallel layer shards the input axis n and reduces partial outputs (Shoeybi et al., 2019). For column-parallel layers, the replicated input allows each rank to construct the same block bases and apply them to its local momentum rows. Under FSDP, our implementation shards each matrix that Dobb updates along its input axis, so each rank holds whole blocks and their momentum columns, as in a row-parallel layer. This construction accommodates both sharding axes under TP and the input-axis sharding under FSDP without requiring cross-shard communication during the optimizer step beyond the data-parallel synchronization of the activation second moments. This synchronization spans FSDP ranks and data-parallel replicas, which process different data, but not the ranks of a TP group, which share their data (Section 5). 

Block partitioning retains a complete basis. The activation spectrum motivates the choice of basis, while sharding under TP and FSDP constrains how we construct and apply it. Dion reduces communication through low-rank updates, restricting each update to a lower-dimensional subspace while retaining the approximation residual through error feedback (Ahn et al., 2025). A rank-r up-date has at least min( m, n ) − r zero singular values, which limits its orthogonality over the full matrix space even when its factors have orthonormal columns. We instead partition the activation second moment into B blocks and retain all eigenvectors within each block. This construction discards cross-block correlations without imposing a reduced-rank constraint on the update and al-lows basis operations to remain local when blocks align with shard boundaries. At B = n, each block holds one input coordinate, each block basis is ±1, and the update reduces to sign( M )/√m,sign descent with momentum (Bernstein et al., 2018). As B increases from 1 to n, Dobb’s update therefore moves from normalization in a single activation eigenbasis toward sign descent with mo-mentum, whereas reducing Dion’s rank increases the number of zero singular values in its update.

# 5 Communication and computation 

We count optimizer-specific cross-shard communication separately from the synchronization of sec-ond moments across data-parallel ranks. All communication counts in this section, including Ta-ble 3, describe logical payloads. We assume the Megatron-LM layout of Section 3 under TP and, under either layout, that each block of Dobb lies within one shard. When neither axis of a matrix is sharded, as under data parallelism alone, cross-shard communication is zero for every optimizer. 

Blocks within shards. For Dobb on a row-parallel layer, or under FSDP with the input-axis shard-ing of Section 3, each input block and its momentum columns reside on the same rank. On a column-parallel layer the input is replicated, so each rank can form every input-side block basis and apply it to its own momentum rows. The m in the sign divisor is known from the parameter shape. No cross-shard statistic is needed for either update. Thus, when each block lies within one shard, Dobb introduces zero optimizer-specific cross-shard bytes, including basis construction, beyond the second-moment synchronization across data-parallel ranks described below. For a square 4096 ×4096 attention projection of Qwen3-8B in fp32, one dense payload is 67.11 MB, and two factors at r = 64 contain 2.10 MB. With each block within one shard, sign normalization contributes 0 MB of cross-shard payload. 

FSDP has a separate synchronization requirement. FSDP ranks process different data, so con-sistent second moments require aggregation across the data-parallel group even when each block lies within one shard. The ranks of a TP group process the same data, so this aggregation does not cross TP ranks. Dobb captures and synchronizes its block second moments only on refresh iterations, once every T optimizer steps; the other optimizer steps issue no optimizer collective. Our implementation reduce-scatters the block second moments across the FSDP ranks, followed by an all-reduce across data-parallel replicas when the FSDP group is replicated. Each rank then refreshes the bases of its own blocks, so no bases are exchanged. # 6 Experiments 

We evaluate whether block-diagonal second moments retain optimization quality as their distributed cost decreases. The principal quality comparison covers GPT-2 Small, Medium, Large, and XL (Radford et al., 2019), with 124M, 354M, 772M, and 1.56B parameters excluding position embed-dings. (2025), Muon, Dion, RMNP, and Dobb multiply their raw updates by the update scale s = τ pmax( m, n ) of Equation 6, with τ = 0 .2,before learning-rate multiplication and weight decay. The purpose of this scale is to bring the update RMS into the range of 0.2 to 0.4 that Liu et al. (2025) observe for AdamW, so that the matrix optimizers can share AdamW’s learning rate and weight decay. For a full-rank orthogonalized m×n

update, their Lemma 1 gives raw RMS 1/pmax( m, n ), so this scale gives Muon an update RMS of 0.2. For the other optimizers, the update RMS depends on the matrix shape and, for Dion, on the rank (Appendix E.1). In our GPT-2 models, the update RMS of RMNP and Dobb is 0.2 or 0.4, also within this range. Dion’s update RMS is 0.2√ρ, which falls from 0.2 at full rank to 0.025 at 

ρ = 1 /64 and lies below this range at every ρ < 1; Appendix E.1 tests a rank-corrected scale that holds it at 0.2. We select AdamW’s learning rate by grid search at Small and Medium and extrapolate a power-law fit of the sweeps to Large and XL (Appendix E.2), then transfer the selected rate and schedule to all other optimizers. All optimizers use weight decay 0.1, following the pretraining setting of Liu et al. (2025).

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduce Dobb, which normalizes momentum coefficients in block-diagonal activation second-moment eigenbases. The central design choice is to discard cross-block correlations while retaining every basis direction within each block. Together with element-wise sign normalization, this con-struction introduces zero optimizer-specific cross-shard bytes when each block lies within one shard, beyond the data-parallel synchronization of the block second moments. Across GPT-2 Small, Medium, Large, and XL, Dobb exhibits less validation-loss degradation under block partitioning than Dion under corresponding rank reductions. Dobb’s optimizer call is shorter than those of Muon and Dion in every measured FSDP and TP configuration (Appendix G). These timing runs are short and report pooled iteration times. Further work could investigate how block size and basis-refresh frequency should vary across lay-ers and training stages. Because Dobb’s basis refresh reads no gradient, it could also move out of the optimizer call into idle intervals of forward-backward computation and communication (Ap-pendix G.2). # F.7 First-micro-batch capture 

The principal policy, first-micro-batch capture, forms the second moment on each refresh iteration from the first micro-batch alone. The alternative, all-micro-batch capture, forms a token-weighted second moment from every micro-batch of that iteration. Both policies use the full mini-batch stochastic gradient for momentum, so they differ only in the data from which the second moment is estimated. Each iteration accumulates gradients over eight micro-batches of 15 sequences on each of the four GPUs, so first-micro-batch capture pools 60 of the iteration’s 480 sequences across GPUs. # F.8 Second-moment and basis precision 

The principal setting accumulates the block second moments in fp32 and stores the bases in bf16. The implementation forms each captured outer product in the activation dtype and casts it to the accumulator dtype; at each refresh it casts the accumulator to fp32 before adding the shift and fac-torizing. Accumulation precision therefore affects the exponential average and its storage, not the factorization arithmetic, while basis precision affects the projection and reconstruction in Equa-tion 5. # G Details of the distributed iteration-time experiments 

This appendix gives the settings and the full timing breakdown of the four campaigns summarized in Table 5. Three campaigns used our implementation, built on PyTorch FSDP2 with DTensor-based TP (Liang et al., 2025): GPT-2 Large and XL on A5000 GPUs (Appendix G.3), GPT-2 XL on H100 GPUs, and GPT-2 3B, 6B, and 8B on H100 GPUs (Appendix G.4). The fourth campaign timed Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B on H100 GPUs with an implementation built on the open-source Dion codebase (Ahn et al., 2025), which we call the Dion-based implementation (Appendix G.5). It also uses PyTorch FSDP2 with DTensor-based TP. For Qwen3, this appendix also reports timings for the output-gradient basis with column normalization, Dobb-G-ℓ2, defined in Algorithm 3. All four campaigns used NCCL over TCP sockets between nodes. The optimizer-call times are measured, and the pooled iteration times are derived from measured means; the communication counts in Section 5 are analytical, and the tables report times only. Pooled iteration time is the comparison metric (Appendix G.1). In every campaign, Dobb refreshes its bases every T = 9 iterations. Both Qwen3 series use the fixed-count partition of Appendix B, with B = FS blocks per weight matrix. Each Qwen3 series pairs Dion’s rank fraction with a width of 1024 /FS , following the width–rank match of Section 5. In the GPT-2 campaigns, Dobb’s blocks follow the shards, so each block lies within one shard. Our implementation applies the matrix optimizers to the two-dimensional weights of the transformer blocks and AdamW to the remaining parameters. It runs a distributed Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) that orthogonalizes each whole weight matrix with Newton–Schulz iterations. Every GPT-2 run started from a random initialization, used a global batch of 480 OpenWebText sequences of 1024 tokens, and kept the gathered FSDP weights across accumulation micro-steps. Our implementation compiles the model with torch.compile , whereas the Dion-based imple-mentation disables model compilation; both log timings on one rank.

# G.2 Work that can leave the optimizer call 

An operation can leave the optimizer call once its inputs are available if it does not require the current mini-batch weight gradient. Table 21 classifies the operations of the compared optimizers and of Shampoo, SOAP, and K-FAC by this criterion. Every operation of AdamW, Muon, and the standard Dion update reads the gradient. The lazy variant of Dion refreshes its right factor only every k steps, and the CPU variant computes this refresh on CPUs from the momentum of earlier steps while forward-backward runs on the GPUs (Ahn et al., 2025, Section 5.1). Shampoo and SOAP accumulate their statistics from the gradient, so their inverse roots or eigenbases can leave the optimizer call only if they use statistics from earlier steps. SOAP recomputes its eigenbases at the end of a step and first uses them in the next (Vyas et al., 2025, Algorithm 3), and Shi et al. (2023) describe a JAX implementation of Shampoo that overlaps the root-inverse computation, offloaded to CPUs, with updates that use stale roots. K-FAC and Dobb estimate their statistics from activations and output gradients instead, so neither their statistics nor the inverses or bases derived from them read the gradient of any step. Martens and Grosse (2015) obtain the K-FAC output-gradient factor from an additional backward pass with sampled targets (their Section 5), recompute the inverses every 20 iterations in their experiments, and note that these computations can run simultaneously with the gradient computation (their Section 8). 

Scheduling Dobb’s work outside the optimizer call. The refresh reads only the block second moments and the previous basis (Algorithm 2), yet both implementations run it inside the optimizer call on refresh iterations. For the activation-side basis, the refresh of a weight matrix could start once its second moment has been captured and reduced, while forward-backward computation continues for other layers. Work moved out of the optimizer call still occupies the GPUs, so it shortens the iteration only to the extent that it fills intervals in which they would otherwise wait; we did not measure this overlap. Osawa et al. (2023) assign the curvature and inversion work of K-FAC to the bubbles of pipeline-parallel schedules and refresh the curvature once every few steps, so that preconditioning is the only added computation. Explicit scheduling of Dobb’s capture, reduction, and basis refresh into idle intervals remains future work.
