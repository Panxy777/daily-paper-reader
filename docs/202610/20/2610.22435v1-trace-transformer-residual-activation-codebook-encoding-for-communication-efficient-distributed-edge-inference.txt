# TRACE: Transformer Residual Activation Codebook Encoding for Communication-efficient Distributed Edge Inference 

Muhammad Azlan Qazi, Alexandros Iosifidis, and Qi Zhang 

Abstract —Distributed Transformer inference across co-located edge devices is fundamentally communication-bound, as exchang-ing activations between sequence-parallel partitions dominates inference latency under commodity edge bandwidths. Existing compression schemes reduce this cost but retain a structural bottleneck: the received data must still undergo key/value pro-jection before attention can proceed, serializing computation behind communication. This paper presents Transformer Resid-ual Activation Codebook Encoding (T RACE ), a framework that addresses these limitations. T RACE places a Vector Quantizer (VQ) at the post-attention residual of every Transformer layer and precomputes codebook-indexed key/value representations. During inference, exchanged activations are encoded into com-pact codebook indices, allowing receivers to directly recover keys and values through table lookup instead of executing the downstream computations required to derive them. By eliminating the received data downstream computation, T RACE 

enables communication–computation overlap and reduces a key latency bottleneck in distributed Transformer inference. A hi-erarchical clustering further structures the learned codebook into a multi-resolution hierarchy, enabling runtime adaptation of the communication bitrate without retraining. The framework supports two operating variants: one prioritizing accuracy and the other balancing accuracy and efficiency through buffer-compatible quantization. Experiments on ViT-Base (ImageNet-1K and CIFAR-100) and ViViT-B (UCF-101) show that T RACE 

achieves up to 1.89% higher accuracy and up to 1.27 × end-to-end inference speedup compared with the state-of-the-art VQ-based distributed inference framework. Compared with uncompressed sequence-parallel inference, T RACE reduces communication over-head by up to 22 ,624 ×.

Index Terms —Distributed inference, edge computing, communication-efficient machine learning, model compression, Transformer neural networks, vector quantization. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# TRANSFORMER architectures have emerged as the dom-inant computational paradigm across vision, language, and multimodal artificial intelligence. Trained at scale, they now define state-of-the-art performance across perception, understanding, and generation tasks [1]–[7]. Their accuracy, however, comes at a steep computational and memory price: a single forward pass through a state-of-the-art Transformer model involves billions of floating-point operations and hun-dreds of megabytes of intermediate activations, making single-device deployment on resource-constrained edge nodes im-practical. Centralized cloud serving is increasingly unsuitable for IoT workloads. End-user latency targets, privacy guarantees,

energy budgets, and unreliable wide-area connectivity all motivate to bring inference at the network edge [8]–[10]. When a single edge device cannot host the model alone, the natural answer is collaborative or distributed inference, in which a model is partitioned across multiple co-located edge nodes whose aggregate compute and memory cover the model’s demands [11]–[13]. Three parallelism families have been developed for this purpose, namely tensor parallelism in Megatron-LM [14], pipeline parallelism in GPipe [15], and sequence parallelism [16]. All three were designed for high-bandwidth datacenter interconnects such as NVLink, where all-reduce and ring activation transfers consume negligible time. The picture changes dramatically at edge bandwidths. In a sequence-parallel deployment with hidden dimension D,sequence length N , and P participating devices, every Trans-former layer must exchange (P −1) N D/P floating-point val-ues per device per layer before the next layer can proceed [11]. At commodity edge bandwidths of tens to a few hundreds Mbps, this volume implies per-layer communication latencies of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, which is several orders of magnitude above local computation time [17]–[19]. Empirical measurements confirm that under bandwidth constraints below 

100 Mbps, inter-device communication accounts for roughly 

60 –93% of total inference latency in distributed BERT exe-cution ( N =1024 , P =4 ), largely negating the speed-up that parallelism is supposed to provide [20]. To address this bottleneck, several lines of work directly compress the communicated payload. Theoretical results in distributed inference [21] establish that combined sparsifica-tion and quantization preserve convergence at extremely low bit budgets, providing the foundation for compressed inter-device communication. ASTRA ’s bitrate is fixed at finetuning time with no mechanism for adaptive resolution. This paper proposes Transformer Residual Activation 

Codebook Encoding (T RACE ), a communication-efficient framework for distributed Transformer inference that closes both gaps simultaneously. The core idea is to place VQ at the post-attention layer boundary (on the hidden state Z produced by the multi-head self-attention sublayer and its first residual addition) and to amortize the next layer’s K and V projections into precomputed lookup buffers indexed by codebook entry, so that the receiver recovers cross-device quantized K/V via direct table lookup with zero matrix-multiply computation. A hierarchical k-medoids clustering [23] of the trained codebook then exposes a multi-resolution sub-codebook hierarchy, al-lowing the deployed model to select bitrate at inference time without retraining. It is imperative to mention that, this paper targets the encoder-only architectures used for perception and understanding tasks (e.g., ViT, ViViT). Extending the mechanism to autoregressive decoder-only LLMs is discussed as future work in Section VI. Across all settings, T RACE closely matches baseline accu-racy at significant communication overhead reduction com-pared with uncompressed sequence-parallel inference meth-ods, and is also superior to the best prior compression methods while enabling seamless compute-communication overlap that those methods cannot provide. The main contributions of this work are:  

> •

Two accuracy–latency variants. TRACE offers two vari-ants that differ in the placement of vector quantiza-tion relative to the post-attention residual (Section V-E). TRACE -nb quantizes before the residual and retains runtime-dependent residual information for higher ac-curacy, whereas T RACE -b quantizes after the residual, enabling deterministic mapping to precomputed key/value entries and compute–communication overlap.  

> •

Post-attention VQ with precomputed K/V buffers. The hidden state Z at the post-attention boundary is quantized using a learned codebook, while the downstream layer normalization, MLP, and K/V representations of each codeword are precomputed during post-training calibra-tion. At inference, only codebook indices are transmitted and cross-device K/V representations are recovered by direct table lookup, eliminating receiver-side matrix mul-tiplication for reconstruction.  

> •

Communication–computation overlap. In TRACE -b, devices transmit codebook indices while concurrently executing local MLP and K/V computations on their available partition data. This removes the receiver-side projection dependency on incoming activations, avoiding the communication–computation serialization that limits prior sequence-parallel methods.  

> •

Multi-resolution bitrate adaptation. TRACE constructs a hierarchical multi-resolution codebook using k-medoids clustering [23] on the trained codebook. Each hierar-chy level forms a valid sub-codebook, enabling runtime bitrate selection without retraining and adaptation to changing bandwidth and resource constraints.  

> •

Modal evaluation. TRACE is evaluated on image clas-sification (ViT-Base/16 on ImageNet-1K and CIFAR-100) and video classification (ViViT-B/16 on UCF-101). A. Transformer Architecture 

The Transformer [24] replaces recurrent and convolutional sequence models with a fully attention-based design. ViT [1] extended the architecture to vision by treating fixed-size image patches as a token sequence. DeiT [25] subsequently improved data efficiency through Transformer-specific knowl-edge distillation. ViViT [2] generalized the design to video by extracting spatio-temporal tube tokens and applying spatial– temporal attention. BERT [26] established the encoder-only bidirectional Transformer paradigm for natural language un-derstanding, while the autoregressive decoder family, including modern LLMs such as Qwen2.5 [3], DeepSeek-R1 [6], and LLaMA [4], [5], has redefined the state of the art on language tasks. T RACE is designed to apply distributed inference across encoder (ViT, ViViT) variants. B. Distributed Transformer Inference at the Edge 

Distributing Transformer inference across multiple devices has been studied primarily in datacenter settings. Tensor parallelism , introduced in Megatron-LM [14], partitions in-dividual weight matrices and inserts an all-reduce collective after each MHSA and MLP sublayer, exchanging hidden-state shards across devices at every sublayer boundary. Pipeline parallelism , exemplified by GPipe [15], assigns contiguous groups of layers to different devices and processes micro-batches in a pipeline, passing the full hidden state at each stage transition. Sequence parallelism [16], [27] distributes the token sequence ( X(ℓ) ∈ RN ×D ) across devices. Each device computes Transformer block for available partitioned sequence X(ℓ)

p ∈ RNp×D , and communicates respected output 

Y(ℓ)

p to other devices. This cycle is repeated for the rest of the Transformer blocks. Pope et al. [18] provide a systematic analysis confirming that, in all three families, inter-device communication is the principal bottleneck at inference stage. This bottleneck becomes even more acute at the network edge, where a growing body of work targets Transformer in-ference under tighter resource constraints. DeTransformer [19] restructures each Transformer layer into decoupled sub-blocks and adaptively places them across edge devices, motivating bandwidth-aware design. EdgeShard [8] addresses LLM par-titioning across heterogeneous edge–cloud nodes through a joint device-selection and shard-placement formulation. TPI-LLM [9] demonstrates serving 70B-parameter models on low-resource edge devices via star-based scheduling and memory windowing. STI [28] elastically pipelines parameter loading and computation on a single mobile device, while Jupiter [10] extends collaborative LLM inference to recent-generation edge hardware. RCIF [29] specifically targets robustness under the highly lossy network conditions characteristic of many IoT de-ployments, a setting in which T RACE ’s ultra-low-bitrate index transmission provides additional resilience against packet loss relative to floating-point transmissions. The works most directly related to T RACE are V OLTAGE ,PRISM , and A STRA , all of which target sequence-parallel distributed Transformer inference but differ in what they compress and where. V OLTAGE [11] is an edge-targeted dis-tributed inference system that exploits position-wise sequence partitioning and adapts the partition strategy to measured band-width and device speed. It serves as the uncompressed base-line, transmitting the full D-dimensional hidden-state partition between devices at every layer boundary with communication volume O(N × D). P RISM [22] replaces full transmission with training-free Segment Means (column-wise averages of token sub-groups) achieving substantial communication re-duction across ViT, BERT, and GPT-2. The receiving device extends its local key and value matrices with the received Segment Means features and applies a scaling-aware softmax correction. Two limitations remain: the transmitted payload is still a D-dimensional float vector per segment, and the receiver must apply Wk/Wv projection matrix multiplications to those vectors before attention can proceed. A STRA [20] goes further by using learned VQ codewords in place of Segment Means. Each device quantizes its pre-attention hidden features against a shared codebook, transmits the assigned codeword indices to its peers, and likewise receives indices for the partitions held by other devices. The received indices are de-referenced through the codebook to reconstruct the corresponding quantized embeddings, from which non-local keys and values Kvq /Vvq are then computed via the Wk/Wv

projection matrices. Each device finally concatenates these reconstructed Kvq /Vvq with the keys and values of its own local partition and performs attention over the combined sequence. However, because VQ is placed before attention, the receiver must still apply the Wk/Wv projection matrix multiplications to every quantized embedding before attention can proceed. This is a non-trivial compute dependency that limits compute–communication overlap and creates a data synchronization bottleneck. A STRA ’s codebook size and bits per vector are also fixed at training time, requiring retraining to adapt to varying link and codebook setups.

A. Lifecycle 

TRACE consists of three phases. First, during offline fine-tuning, a layer-specific vector quantizer is inserted at each post-attention residual. An input-stage quantizer VQ 0 is also used so that the first inter-device exchange is represented by codebook indices. The model and codebooks are jointly finetuned for the target task. Second, after finetuning, the model weights and codebooks are frozen. Each trained codebook is organized into a nested hierarchy of sub-codebooks using hierarchical k-medoids clus-tering. This provides multiple resolution levels from a single trained model, allowing the communication data size to be selected according to the available bandwidth, latency bud-get, and desired accuracy. A one-time calibration procedure then computes, for every codeword, the K/V representations required by the subsequent attention layer and stores them in the corresponding Kvqcb and Vvqcb lookup buffers. Since each sub-codebook contains entries from the original trained codebook, all resolution levels reuse the same buffers without recalibration. Third, during online inference, an active resolution level is selected and each device quantizes its local post-attention residual using the corresponding sub-codebook. The device transmits only the resulting indices, which its peers use to retrieve the corresponding rows from the precomputed K/V buffers. These non-local K/V representations are combined with the locally computed K/V representations for global attention. The finetuning procedure, hierarchy construction, buffer calibration, and distributed inference operations are detailed in Section IV.

We also evaluate T RACE -nb ( non-buffered ), which places VQ before the post-attention residual and uses no input-stage quantizer VQ 0 (Case 1 A.i in Table VI). Its first inter-device communication therefore carries the unquantized input partition, while subsequent exchanges transmit codeword indices but reconstruct representations that also depend on runtime residuals. The precomputed K/V buffers are consequently invalid, and the receiver must apply the corresponding transformation chain to the received representations before K/V projection. TRACE -nb thus preserves runtime residual information and achieves higher accuracy. In contrast, T RACE -b replaces this computation with buffer lookup, enabling compute–communication overlap. TRACE -nb is therefore preferable when the application does not have stringent inference latency constraints and the connectivity between edge devices offers high bandwidth, whereas T RACE -b is preferable when application has stringent inference latency constraints and the network connection is bandwidth-constrained or highly variable. C0 ⊂ C 1 ⊂ · · · ⊂ C R = C, K0 < K 1 < · · · < K R = K. 

(18) We use (K0, K 1, K 2, K 3, K 4) = (2 , 4, 8, 32 , 1024) . Con-struction proceeds fine-to-coarse: starting from the full code-book CR, k-medoids is applied to select K3=32 representative medoids; those 32 medoids are then clustered to yield K2=8 ,then K1=4 , and finally K0=2 at the coarsest level. We evaluate TRACE on image and video classification, comparing against the uncompressed baseline (V OLTAGE ), Segment-Means method (P RISM ), and the strongest prior VQ-based method (A STRA ). Section V-A describes the setup; Sections V-D–V-B present accuracy, latency and scalability, communication, compression results; and Section V-E reports four ablations isolating the contribution of each design choice. 

A. Experimental Setup a) Models and datasets: For image classification, ViT-Base/16 [1] is evaluated on ImageNet-1K [33] (single-device baseline accuracy 80 .32% ) and CIFAR-100 [34] ( 92 .30% ). For video classification, ViViT-B/16 [2] is evaluated on UCF-101 [35] (90 .77% ), with scalability measured for P ∈{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10 } devices and evaluated across its multi-resolution sub-codebook hierarchy K ∈ { 2, 4, 8, 32 , 1024 }.

b) Training & Evaluation: VQ codebooks are finetuned jointly with the pretrained backbone model using EMA code-book updates ( α=0 .99 ), commitment weight β=0 .1, local-KV drop ρ=0 .3, and K=1024 codewords initialized by k-means on the first batch. The multi-resolution hierarchy is built post-training by k-medoids. Compute latency is measured on a CPU configured with two cores, and communication latency is computed at a range of network bandwidth BW =

{1, 2, 4, 8, 10 , 20 } Mbps. All latency figures report effective per-device latency averaged over fifty runs ( ±5 ms standard deviation). 

B. Compression Ratio 

Fig. 7 reports the compression ratio (CR), defined as the ratio of uncompressed intermediate features to compressed transmitted data. For ViT at P =2 , T RACE (K=1024 ) achieves 

2,426 × compression versus A STRA ’s 1,528 × at the same codebook size, which is 1.59 × improvement that stems from TRACE transmitting ⌈log 2 K⌉ bits per token independently of model’s feature dimension. Successive resolution levels yield near-proportional CR gains: 4,813 × (K=32 ), 7,937 ×

(K=8 ), and 22 ,624 × (K=2 ). Fig. 8 confirms that these ratios are essentially invariant to device count for ViViT: across 

P ∈ { 2, 4, 10 }, the CR for each resolution level varies by less than 0.5% , indicating that the compression mechanism does not degrade as more devices participate, as long as the number of tokens is sufficient large in each partition. 

C. Inference Latency 

Figs. 5a and 5b show the effective per-device inference latency of ViT-Base/16 at 10 Mbps, decomposed into com-putation and communication, while Table IV reports the cor-responding measurements. Fig. 6 further evaluates communi-cation latency over the 1–20 Mbps bandwidth range. Together, the results show that T RACE -b reduces both communication overhead and receiver-side computation, resulting in the lowest effective latency among the evaluated methods. At P =2 , T RACE -b requires only 0.12 –1.14 ms of com-munication by transmitting compact codeword indices, de-pending on the resolution m. Unlike A STRA , T RACE -b also avoids receiver-side K/V projections by retrieving cross-device K/V vectors from precomputed buffers. Consequently, its computation latency decreases to 92 ms at P =2 and 

63 ms at P =3 for K≤32 . At K=1024 , the larger nearest-neighbor search increases computation modestly to 106 ms and 

72 ms, respectively. In comparison, A STRA requires 1.81 ms of communication, while P RISM reduces communication to 

140 .63 ms through Segment-Means compression, although its communication time still exceeds its computation time at 10 Mbps. The uncompressed V OLTAGE baseline incurs 

2,770 .31 ms of communication at 10 Mbps, approximately 

30 × its 92 .5 ms computation latency, making communication the dominant contributor to end-to-end latency. The communication advantage is maintained across the evaluated bandwidth range in Fig. 6. As bandwidth decreases, the latency of all methods increases, but the effect is most severe for methods that transmit high-dimensional features. 11

D. Accuracy versus Compression 

Table III and Figs. 4a–4b show the accuracy–compression trade-off on ImageNet-1K and CIFAR-100. At K=1024 , both TRACE variants outperform the prior compression baselines, ASTRA and P RISM , across all four settings. T RACE -nb con-sistently achieves higher accuracy, for example, 80 .23% on ImageNet-1K and 91 .23% on CIFAR-100 at P =2 , which is 1.78 and 1.89 percentage point higher than A STRA . T RACE -b still achieves 1.45 and 1.21 percentage point higher accuracy than A STRA at P =2 . The trade-off is that T RACE -nb preserves higher accuracy, while T RACE -b trades a modest accuracy loss for exact buffer-based K/V reconstruction and compute– communication overlap, achieving inference acceleration. This difference highlights the accuracy–efficiency trade-off intro-duced by the post-residual placement required for buffer precomputation. a) Implications and limitations: The results in Section V indicate that removing the receiver’s compute dependency is what drives T RACE ’s latency gains over P RISM and A STRA ,and that a single multi-resolution codebook lets a deployed model react to changing link conditions without retraining. 14

b) Extension to decoder-only LLMs: TRACE ’s buffers and split-attention (Eq. (16)) assume unconditional cross-device attendance, which holds for the bidirectional encoders evaluated here but not for autoregressive decoder-only LLMs such as Qwen2.5 [3]: causal masking would require T RACE ’s distributed attention mechanism to be made partition- and position-aware, and autoregressive decoding would addition-ally require a scheduling strategy that allocates compute het-erogeneously across devices to manage the differing prefill and decode phases of concurrent requests. These are architectural changes rather than a direct application of the present design, so we did not implement or evaluate T RACE on decoder-only LLMs in this work and leave this extension to future work. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented TRACE , a communication-efficient framework for distributed Transformer inference across edge devices. T RACE introduces multi-resolution vector quantiza-tion to substantially reduce cross-device communication while consistently improving inference accuracy over the state-of-the-art VQ-based method A STRA . The framework further pro-vides two operating variants. T RACE -nb prioritizes accuracy, achieving up to 1.89% higher accuracy than A STRA , whereas TRACE -b achieves up to 1.45% higher accuracy and up to 

1.27 × inference speedup over A STRA by introducing buffer-compatible quantization to precompute codebook-indexed K/V representations, thereby eliminating receiver-side K/V pro-jection and enabling communication–computation overlap. In addition, a multi-resolution hierarchical codebook clustering enables runtime bitrate adaptation without retraining. Across ViT-Base/16 and ViViT-B/16 on image and video classification tasks, T RACE achieves approximately 22 ,624 × and 24 ,500 ×

reduction in transmitted activation payload, respectively, rel-ative to uncompressed sequence parallelism. Together, these results demonstrate that T RACE provides a flexible foundation for communication-efficient distributed Transformer inference, while enabling low-latency execution through precomputed K/V lookup and communication–computation overlap.
