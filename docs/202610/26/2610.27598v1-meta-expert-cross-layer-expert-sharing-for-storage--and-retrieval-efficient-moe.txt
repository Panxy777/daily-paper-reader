## ABSTRACT 

As model parameters scale up, sparsely activated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) ap-proaches have become essential for model scaling. However, mainstream models currently adopt hierarchical architectures, where experts in different layers, al-though topologically independent, exhibit substantial similarity and redundancy in their stored content. Compared to the hierarchically arranged Local Expert in existing mainstream models, we hypothesize that inter-layer shared Meta Expert 

hold greater potential. To this end, we conduct a multi-dimensional empirical comparison between Local and Meta Experts, and find that models incorporat-ing Meta Experts exhibit lower parameter redundancy, together with lower cross-entropy loss and higher downstream task accuracy. With the activated quantity and the expert granularity held fixed, our model matches the performance of Vanilla MoE using only 70–80% of the experts , substantially improving the model’s scaling potential. Beyond removing inter-layer redundancy among experts and improving information storage efficiency, our theoretical analysis further shows that, compared with Local Experts, Meta Experts provide hidden states with more flexible residual pathways, thereby improving information retrieval efficiency.

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have gradually approached or even surpassed human-level ability in general text, mathematics, and code (Brown et al., 2020; OpenAI et al., 2024). This progress owes much to the increasing scale of model parameters and training data: intelligence has been improved substantially as both factors grow (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022b).

1arXiv:2610.27598v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 Preprint To enable ever-larger models, parameter sparsification has become an important approach (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Muennighoff et al., 2025). As the module that accounts for the vast majority of model parameters, the feed-forward network was originally a densely computed block. (Vaswani et al., 2017a). However, as model size grows, such dense computation gradu-ally becomes prohibitively expensive, creating efficiency bottlenecks for both training and infer-ence (Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022). Meanwhile, although feed-forward networks are densely computed, only a sparse subset of parameters is actually activated for a given token dur-ing its forward pass (Li et al., 2023; Mirzadeh et al., 2024); the remaining inactive parameters still participate in the computation, leading to substantial waste. To address this, researchers have proposed designing feed-forward networks with a natively sparse-activation structure, most notably the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). MoE splits the feed-forward network into multiple sub-networks along the intermediate dimension and routes each token to only a limited number of them before computation. This design endows the model with a large total parameter count while ensuring that each token’s forward pass computation uses only a small fraction of parameters, reconciling scale with efficiency. With the advent of increasingly sparse MoE models, parameter efficiency along the width dimen-sion has been steadily improved (Dai et al., 2024). In this work, however, we ask how parameter efficiency can be improved along the depth dimension (Raposo et al., 2024). On the one hand, inter-pretability studies suggest that feed-forward networks store relatively static knowledge (Geva et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2022), and that different layers may store similar knowledge, suggesting nontriv-ial redundancy across layers (Meng et al., 2022; Takase & Kiyono, 2023; Fan et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2025; Men et al., 2025). On the other hand, Looped Transformers reuse the same parame-ters across recurrent applications of a block (Dehghani et al., 2018; Giannou et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2019; Csord´ as et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026a),and recent work shows that this design can attain the reasoning performance of much larger models with far fewer parameters (Bae et al., 2025; Saunshi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). This benefit, however, comes with additional recursions and greater serial depth, incurring higher computational and latency costs. To balance parameter storage efficiency and computational cost, this work explores the potential of another Parameter Loop scheme — Cross-Layer Expert Sharing. Unlike Looped Transformers, which keep the total parameters of each layer unchanged but increase the number of recursions, we aim to keep the number of recursions unchanged while increasing the candidate experts of each layer, and use the sparse activation of MoE to keep both the activated parameters and the computation per layer unchanged. We refer to the experts shared across layers as Meta Experts , and the layer-private experts in the conventional design as Local Experts .Our experiments show that, compared with the Local Expert of hierarchical architectures, Meta Experts reduce parameter redundancy and achieve lower cross-entropy loss together with higher downstream task accuracy. At a fixed number of activated experts and expert granularity, our model achieves comparable performance to Vanilla MoE with only 70% as many experts, indicating a substantially higher effective capacity per parameter. Further analysis reveals another reason why cross-layer expert sharing is effective: Meta Experts provide hidden states with more flexible paths for storage and retrieval. Compared with hierarchical architectures, cross-layer expert sharing im-plicitly provides more flexible residual connections: beyond the forward residual connections, it introduces a backward residual connection at the activation level—one that operates within a single forward pass and is therefore absent from existing models. Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) A systematic analysis of the effect of Meta Experts on model performance, covering their activation and total-number ratios among all experts, their presence in every layer, and their partial hierarchical organization. Although these configurations moderately affect performance, Meta Experts consistently bring significant improvements. (2) An explanation of why Meta Experts are effective from the perspective of residual connections. By comparing the forward hidden-state updates and the backward parameter updates of models with and without Meta Experts, we show that this design grants shallow and deep layers bidirectional access to representations across depth, enabling more flexible information retrieval. 2Preprint Layer l + 1

We next investigate the effect of the topology of Meta/Local Experts on model performance. On the one hand, we explore whether every layer should include Meta Experts; on the other hand, we explore whether the Meta Experts should themselves be partially hierarchically organized. 

Settings. On the one hand, we consider variants in which Local Experts are used exclusively, with no Meta Experts introduced at all: either in only the first 1, 2, 4, or 8 layers of the model, in only the last 1, 2, 4, or 8 layers, or in both the first and the last 1, 2, or 4 layers. On the other hand, we consider partitioning the Meta Experts into 2, 4, or 8 sub-pools, which are then accessed in a looped manner—either in a chunked or an interleaved fashion. 

Pilot Experiments (Fig 6). It can be observed that both operations—introducing Local-Experts-only layers, and organizing the Meta Experts hierarchically so as to mimic the topology of Local Experts—generally incur a loss degradation, which further demonstrates the positive role of Meta Experts in model performance. Notably, under a lighter degree of intervention, the loss slightly im-proves: for example, when only the first two and the last two layers are restricted to Local Experts, or when the Meta Experts are looped only twice. This suggests, on the one hand, that experts in the shallowest and deepest layers serve more differentiated functions, with essentially no mutual redun-dancy between these layers—a conclusion also supported by the expert redundancy measurements. On the other hand, mildly hierarchical Meta Experts help improve the model capability slightly; hi-erarchy, however, introduces a certain amount of expert redundancy, and why this redundancy does not translate into a higher loss remains an open question for further investigation.

## 4 META EXPERTS AS BIDIRECTIONAL RESIDUAL PATHWAYS 

Consider a stack of L MoE layers with hidden size H. Writing xl for the residual stream entering the MoE sub-layer of layer l, ˆxl = RMSNorm( xl), and γ for the residual scaling coefficient, the sub-layer updates the stream as 

xl+1 = xl + γ E θl (ˆ xl; Rl), (3) where Eθl is an expert block of N SwiGLU experts, each with parameter dimension d, Rl is the router, and top-k routing activates k experts per token. In a layer-wise MoE, θl and Rl are layer-private; in Meta Experts, a single block θ and router R are learned once and shared by all layers. 4.1 LAYER -WISE MOE. 

Forward (read). The expert of layer l injects its output into the residual stream at layer l (Eq. equa-tion 3) and propagates only forward: hidden state xm can contain layer l’s content only if l < m , and even then only as a frozen component, because the readout operator of layer m (Eθm , R m) differs from the write operator of layer l (Eθl , R l). 

Training (write). The residual connection does run backward as a gradient highway: the adjoint 

¯xl = ∂L/∂x l inherits ¯xl+1 through the identity branch with unit gain, so the training signal of deeper layers does reach shallower parameters θl without attenuation. This channel, however, is parameter-level rather than activation-level, lands in layer-private parameters that only layer l ever reads, and over the m − l hops between a pair of layers l < m , the deep-layer component is progressively mixed into the composite adjoint, diluted by the training signals of all intermediate layers rather than arriving at θl as an isolated, addressable contribution. 

Net effect. In one forward pass, a hidden state includes only earlier layers’ expert content; deeper layers’ signal reaches a shallow layer solely as a parameter update during training, never as content in its readout. 4.2 MOE WITH META EXPERTS .

## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we present an empirical and theoretical analysis of cross-layer expert sharing in MoE models, contrasting the hierarchically arranged Local Experts of standard architectures with inter-layer shared Meta Experts . Our systematic comparisons—over the Meta/Local ratio, the activation ratio, and the topology of the shared pool—show that Meta Experts consistently reduce expert re-dundancy and cross-entropy loss while improving downstream task accuracy: at a fixed number of activated experts and expert granularity, models built on shared experts match the performance of Vanilla MoE with only 70–80% of the expert budget, substantially improving scaling potential. Beyond these empirical observations, our analysis shows that a shared expert block is not merely a parameter-saving device: it widens the forward residual connections and grants hidden states bidirectional access to expert content across depth, improving both the storage and the retrieval of 9Preprint information. Taken together, our results suggest that inter-layer redundancy, long treated as an in-trinsic cost of deep MoE models, can instead be converted into effective capacity. Future directions include extending this analysis to larger models and broader architectures, and understanding why mildly hierarchical shared experts introduce redundancy without degrading performance. ## A APPENDIX 

A.1 RELATED WORK 

Weight Tying was originally introduced to share parameters between the embedding layer and the language model head (Press & Wolf, 2017; Inan et al., 2016): since the two matrices map tokens to and from the hidden space, tying them nearly halves the corresponding parameters while often improving perplexity, and the practice has become standard in modern language models (Vaswani et al., 2017b; Raffel et al., 2020). Beyond tying modules at the same depth, weight tying can be pushed further, to reusing parameters across depth (Dehghani et al., 2018; Lan et al., 2019). 

Looped Transformer pushes weight tying to extreme: the same block is applied multiple times per token, unrolling a small set of weights into arbitrary depth (Dehghani et al., 2018; Giannou et al., 15 Preprint 2023; Hua et al., 2025). This line of work shows that weight reuse across depth can substitute for pa-rameter growth, and that a compact recurrent model can match the reasoning performance of much larger ones (Bae et al., 2025; Saunshi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), at the cost of additional recur-sions and a higher degree of serialization. Meta Experts share the same motivation—extracting more effective capacity from a fixed parameter budget—but apply tying at a finer granularity and within a single feed-forward pass: only the expert block is reused across layers, without extra recursions, so training and inference retain the parallelism of standard Transformers, while attention may remain layer-specific. Depending on what is reused across depth, this paradigm subsumes several lines of work. 

Attn Weight Sharing reuses the attention projections (or entire attention blocks) of different layers, yielding moderate parameter savings at little accuracy cost (Xiao et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2019; Mu et al., 2026). These results suggest that the per-layer parameter budget of Transformers is partially redundant—but leave open where the redundancy concentrates and whether the same principle ap-plies to the rest of the block. Attn KV-Cache Sharing reuses not parameters but activations: in long-context or serving scenarios, multiple layers attend to a common KV cache, trading represen-tational independence for inference memory and throughput (Sun et al., 2024; Brandon et al., 2024; Zuhri et al., 2025; Wu & Tu, 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Such sharing is typically applied at deployment time, affects the cache rather than the learned weights, and is often found to be nearly lossless— suggesting that layers can tolerate reading the same content, but not that they should store the same content. Meta Experts operate on the storage side: the shared expert pool is written by every layer during training, so cross-layer sharing reshapes what the model stores and how hidden states retrieve it, rather than merely reusing cached activations at inference. 

FFN Weight Sharing is the closest line to our work: it reuses feed-forward parameters across layers, either by tying the FFN of all layers or by sharing it among a subset (Lan et al., 2019; Pires et al., 2023; Thawakar et al., 2024). Existing studies, however, focus on dense FFNs and report mixed results, and sharing is typically applied uniformly to every layer. MoE Weight Sharing has been explored in several recent works: experts are shared across layers, but almost always under an every-layer sharing topology, whose communication and memory overhead is difficult to scale (Li et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Jiao et al., 2026). Meta Experts generalize this idea to a more flexible regime: a shared pool of experts is accessed through several topology that is more friendly for communication and memory (reserving boundary layers for local experts, sub-pool partitioning with chunked or interleaved access), allows the model to decide which content is worth sharing across depth—and, as our analysis shows, implicitly turns the shared experts into bidirectional residual pathways between layers. Hyperparameter selection. We do not tune the training hyperparameters per architectural variant. Instead we fix a single compute-optimal configuration for the vanilla baseline backbone at each rung and reuse it unchanged for every Meta Experts variant. The baseline configuration—the joint depth–width scaling, and the per-depth global batch size, peak learning rate, and token budget— is obtained by sweeping these settings and retaining the compute-optimal choice at each depth, following established practice on compute-optimal training and its schedule (Hoffmann et al., 2022a; H¨ agele et al., 2024) and on scaling laws for the learning rate and batch size (Bi et al., 2024; Team et al., 2025). The Mixture-of-Experts structural choices (expert count, granularity, and the shared-expert ratio) are likewise set in line with recent MoE scaling-law analyses (Tian et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025). Because every Meta Experts variant inherits the baseline configuration without re-tuning, a difference in loss at a rung reflects the architectural change rather than a difference in hyperparameters; this reuse is further supported by evidence that optimal learning rate and batch size are governed primarily by the compute budget and are largely insensitive to Mixture-of-Experts architectural variation (Team et al., 2025). Moreover, at a given depth every variant keeps the per-token top-8 routing and hence the same activated parameters, and shares the same token budget, so all variants at a rung incur the same training FLOPs and are plotted at a single shared compute coordinate; the compute-versus-loss comparison at that rung is thus iso-FLOP by construction, and the only free axis across variants is the loss.
