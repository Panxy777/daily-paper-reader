## Abstract 

We introduce FORGE (Fast On-chip Rotor Generation for Experts), a procedural weight generation system for Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) inference. Rather than storing and loading every expert weight tensor from HBM, we show how a compact accelerator inside each Streaming Multipro-cessor can regenerate them on-the-fly from 𝑂 (log 2 𝑑 ) ro-tor parameters per projection. Per replaced layer, gigabytes of stored expert weights are approximated with under a megabyte of seeds. This addresses the dual memory bottle-neck of autoregressive Sparse MoE decode, where per-expert FFN operations are HBM-bandwidth-bound at small routed-token counts and the aggregate expert footprint forces multi-node Expert Parallelism (EP) with all-to-all traffic. We realize the approach as the Rotor Generation Unit (RGU), a fixed-function datapath that generates FFN weight tiles without competing with Tensor Core execution. With 30% of the MoE FFN layers replaced across five Sparse MoE models, we estimate 1 .3–2 .8× higher system throughput at matched GPU count and 1 .10–1 .15 × lower per-user decode latency at short context. There is broad consensus that sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures [18, 38, 58] are now the dominant design for frontier open-weight LLMs. It turns out that this archi-tecture amplifies the bottlenecks of short-context decode along three distinct axes. All three lead back to one architec-tural fact: expert weights are the overwhelming majority of the parameter footprint, i.e., 94–98% across the five frontier MoEs in Table 1, and constantly occupy HBM capacity. 

(P1) HBM bandwidth bottleneck at small effective batch. We know that MoE top-𝐾 routing assigns each token to 𝐾 of 𝐸 total experts, e.g., 𝐾 =8 of 𝐸 =256 for DeepSeek-v3.2 [15]; 𝐾 =10 of 𝐸 =512 for Qwen3-Coder-Next [4]. But a serving batch of 𝐵 tokens therefore yields only 𝐵𝐾 /𝐸 tokens per expert under uniform routing. At 𝐵 =32 on DeepSeek-v3.2, each expert sees a single token, collapsing its Up/Gate/Down projections to GEMVs. Per-token decode latency then be-comes dominated by HBM weight transfer, leaving the Ten-sor Cores idle [52]. 

(P2) HBM capacity bottleneck. At FP8, total MoE weight footprints range from 31GB (Qwen3-30B [54]) to 742GB (GLM-5 [24]). All but the smallest exceed the 80GB HBM of a single H100. While offloading weights to host memory is an alternative, it severely bottlenecks decode performance to PCIe bandwidth limits [14]. This forces Expert Parallelism (EP) [30, 38, 55] across multiple GPUs and across multiple nodes for large models—paying a cross-node all-to-all for every decode step. Now, within each GPU, weights also con-sume HBM that would otherwise serve KV cache, capping the number of concurrent users per GPU. The capacity prob-lem thus has two consequences: deployment topology (how many GPUs, how much interconnect) and serving concur-rency (how many users per GPU). 

(P3) Compression does not change the access pattern. 

Quantization [20, 40, 45] (FP8 on Hopper, INT4 variants) is the standard response to address both pressures. It does shrink per-parameter bytes, but the GPU must still fetch every activated weight from HBM on each decode step and reserve HBM capacity for the weights. Quantization thus reduces the bandwidth bottleneck and lowers the capacity ceiling, but we observe it eliminates neither. More aggressive schemes (e.g., INT4) push this further along the same axis without changing the underlying access pattern. We observe that (P1), (P2), and (P3) all derive from a single architectural assumption that is rarely challenged: expert weights are a static payload resident in HBM. 

Our approach. The main idea is to instead treat each expert projection as approximately procedurally synthesiz-able (i.e., the end product of a recipe rather than a stored tensor) so that, in principle, all three problems can be ad-dressed at once . Concretely, recent work by Pence et al. [51] on a Clifford-algebra object, rotors, offers the theoretical feasibility: 𝑂 (𝑑 2) linear projections can be approximated by 𝑂 (log 2 𝑑 ) rotor parameters. Operationally, those parame-ters are angles, and the projection is rebuilt by applying the corresponding rotations in a fixed sequence. We take this, with appropriate adjustments, from a theo-retical formalism to hardware. Under our construction, each pre-trained expert in a replaced MoE layer is fit offline to a rotor-parameter seed, and at inference the seed stands in for the dense expert tensor for that layer. A fixed-function 

Rotor Generation Unit (RGU) inside each Streaming Mul-tiprocessor materializes weight tiles on demand for those to-be-replaced layers and writes them directly into Shared Memory, feeding the Tensor Core pipeline without an HBM weight load on the replaced path (Figure 1). Against (P1), on replaced layers this swaps the HBM weight-loading path for a dedicated on-chip generator, shifting the per-expert MoE FFN kernel from bandwidth-bound to compute-bound. Against (P2), replaced-layer expert storage collapses by more than three orders of magnitude, to under a megabyte of parameter seeds per layer (Table 1), freeing close to a third of the weight HBM for expanded KV cache and shrinking the minimum-fit GPU count for the largest models enough to eliminate cross-node deployment. Against (P3), the gains are orthogonal to quantization, as we remove the transfer rather than compress it. The net effect is higher system throughput without giving up per-user latency, which normally trade off against each other, at a cost in downstream accuracy. ## 2 Background and Motivation 

2.1 Sparse MoE Architecture 

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures [18, 38, 58] replace the single dense FFN in each transformer block with a set of 𝐸 expert FFN modules and a learned gating net-work. At each MoE layer, the gating network routes each token to a small subset of experts—typically 8 out of 256 (DeepSeek-v3.2 [15], GLM-5 [24]) or 10 out of 512 (Qwen3-Coder-Next [4]). Each activated expert executes a standard SwiGLU FFN [57]: three linear projections (Up, Gate, Down) mapping between the model’s hidden dimension 𝑑 and the per-expert intermediate dimension 𝑑 𝑚𝑜𝑒 . Only the activated experts contribute to the forward pass, keeping per-token FLOPs comparable to a dense model of equivalent depth. While each expert is compact (small 𝑑 𝑚𝑜𝑒 ), the total ex-pert count drives aggregate parameter volume: MoE weight parameters constitute the dominant share of total model footprint across frontier architectures. Table 1 summarizes the five target models evaluated in this work. At FP8 pre-cision, MoE parameters account for 94–98% of total model size, ranging from 29 GB (Qwen3-30B [54], 128 experts) to 728 GB (GLM-5, 256 experts across 75 MoE layers). This pa-rameter concentration is the source of both the bandwidth and capacity bottlenecks analyzed in Section 2.2.

In autoregressive decode, each token generates a single out-put, reducing linear projections to GEMV-class operations with arithmetic intensity [65] proportional to batch size [52]. While Sparse MoE reduces active FLOPs per token, it in-creases total parameter capacity—often exceeding 600 GB for frontier models. Fitting these parameters in HBM forces multi-node Expert Parallelism (EP) [30, 38, 55], where each decode step incurs cross-node all-to-all token routing. This communication overhead scales with EP degree and persists even with high-bandwidth interconnects (NVLink within a node, InfiniBand across nodes). The resulting deployment topology simultaneously consumes HBM capacity that could otherwise serve KV cache scaling and introduces per-token networking latency that compounds across MoE layers. 

Alternatives? Quantization (FP8, FP4, INT4) [20, 40, 45] reduces per-parameter storage/transfer cost. It does not change the core access pattern: the GPU must still load every weight from HBM for every token. Our FP8 baseline already uses the narrowest weight format which the H100’s Tensor Cores support natively. FORGE is orthogonal to this axis rather than competing with it: it synthesizes the weights of a lin-ear map and is agnostic to the bit resolution at which those weights are stored, so it composes on top of whatever quan-tization a deployment already uses. The question shifts from how much smaller a weight can be made, to whether it has to be fetched at all. 

2.3 Rotor Projection: Decouple Storage and Compute Intuition. Consider the transformation of a vector via a linear map. The de facto approach is to represent this weight matrix explicitly. If we instead view the global transform as a composition of elementary simpler transformations (e.g., rotations), then we only need to store the specific angles that define them. Pence et al. [51] established that standard linear projections can be replaced and approximated by ro-tors, structured transformations built from Clifford algebra, achieving competitive downstream performance in LLM at-tention layers with only 𝑂 (log 2 𝑑 ) learned parameters com-pared to the 𝑂 (𝑑 2) of dense matrices. 

Rotors and Multivectors. We know that a rotation ma-trix acts on vectors. A rotor simply extends this action to the full multivector space of the algebra, naturally lifting the rotation to operate on the higher-order components. For a more complete introduction, see [51]. The rotor construction replaces a dense 2 𝑛 × 2𝑛 weight matrix with a block-diagonal structured matrix, where 𝑑 = 2𝑛 is the input dimension. Challenge. Even after we bound the theoretical workload by adopting 𝑚 =2 for all rotor projections (Section 5.7 evalu-ates its effect on accuracy), we find that systems performance is still bottlenecked. Generating these parallelized weights on-the-fly via standard pipelines ends up starving the Tensor Cores and also creates difficulties for the GPU’s instruction scheduler. To adjust the theory to a performant systems ar-chitecture, this section derives the parallel execution model from the initial inherently sequential generation concept (Section 2.3). Then, we discuss the hardware mandate it im-plies: why a software-only generation approach hurts the Tensor Cores it is meant to supply. Key Architectural Insight: While the mathematical ro-tor formulation is inherently sequential in its outer loop, its inner combinatorial updates exhibit strict, static data independence. Our fundamental insight is that this static independence can be mapped to a dedicated, collision-free hardware scheduler. This allows us to bypass the massive dynamic scheduling overhead that makes a software-only (SIMT) approach not viable, enabling on-chip weight gener-ation that perfectly overlaps with Tensor Core execution.

The RGU Solution: To eliminate this contention, we decouple the generation workload from the SIMT pipeline entirely. We introduce the Rotor Generation Unit (RGU), a compact, fixed-function accelerator embedded within each Streaming Multiprocessor. By utilizing a dedicated pipelined FMA datapath and a local SRAM with a precomputed conflict-free schedule, the RGU bypasses dynamic scheduling bot-tlenecks and requires no shared SIMT resources. Upon re-ceiving a command with the target (𝑛, 𝑘 ) configuration and rotor seeds, the RGU generates a complete weight Supertile directly into SMEM. This on-chip generation replaces the conventional HBM weight-loading path while remaining fully transparent to the Tensor Cores (Figure 5).

4.3 Supertile SRAM & Processing Element 

Problem: Sustaining dual-pair processing throughput requires four simultaneous SRAM accesses (2 reads, 2 writes) per cy-cle slot. Implementing this with multi-ported SRAM would severely bloat the SM area overhead. Solution: We achieve 4-port performance using compact 1R1W banks by exploiting the static predictability of the rotor accesses. Rather than employing expensive multi-ported SRAM, the design uses 4-bank interleaving (depth 116 per bank) to synthesize 2R2W effective bandwidth from four simple 1R1W banks. Total capacity: 462 × 512-bit = 28 .875 KB (FP8), sized for the largest 𝑘 -block (  11 5

=462 rows). Collision freedom is guaranteed by a precomputed pair schedule (described below), eliminating dynamic bank arbitration entirely. Each of the two 64-wide SIMD datapaths implements a 3-cycle pipelined FMA split as two multiply stages followed by an add, halving the critical combinational depth. The two multiply stages read 𝑟 𝑖 and 𝑟 𝑗 from SRAM on successive cy-cles and compute the four cross-products required by Eq. (2); the add stage commits the rotated row pair 𝑊 [𝑖 ], 𝑊 [ 𝑗 ]. The add of the previous row pair overlaps with the first multiply of the current pair, sustaining 2-cycle pair throughput from the 3-cycle pipeline (Figure 8). 

Static Collision-Free Scheduling: The dual-pair datap-ath requires two independent row-pair indices per 2-cycle slot, which would otherwise demand dynamic bank arbitra-tion with cycle-level conflict detection. Instead, a precom-puted Schedule ROM stores collision-free pair orderings for all (𝑛, 𝑘 ) configurations with 𝑛 =9. . . 11, generated offline via maximum matching over the bank-conflict graph. A single table serves every configuration, eliminating runtime arbi-tration entirely.

## 5 Evaluation 

5.1 Methodology 

Baseline per-expert MoE FFN execution times are obtained from NVIDIA Nsight Compute (NCU) profiling of cuBLASLt FP8 GEMMs on an H100 SXM5 GPU (132 SMs, 3.35 TB/s HBM3, CUDA 13.2). For each model, we profile the three expert projections (Up, Gate, Down) at their native dimen-sions and extract per-tile compute time, memory transfer time, and achieved bandwidth utilization. Rotor execution times are derived from the RTL-verified cycle model of the RGU (Section 4), converting generation cycles to wall-clock time at the achieved synthesis frequency. We do not evaluate a narrower weight format: FP8 is the narrowest the H100’s Tensor Cores support natively (Section 2.2). We evaluate five Sparse MoE architectures spanning three chunk dimensions: DeepSeek-v3.2 and GLM-5 ( 𝑛 =11, 256 ex-perts), Qwen3-235B ( 𝑛 =10, 128 experts), and Qwen3-30B and Qwen3-Coder-Next ( 𝑛 =9, 128/512 experts). Kernel-level eval-uation targets the memory-bound decode regime; system-level analysis (Section 5.5) extends to higher serving batch sizes where freed HBM capacity drives throughput gains independent of per-kernel speedup. We report all system re-sults at 𝑟 =0.3, chosen as a conservative point on the accuracy-performance tradeoff (Section 5.7). The RGU is implemented in SystemVerilog as an FP8 dual-pair architecture processing two independent row pairs per 2-cycle slot. Standard cells are synthesized using Synopsys Design Compiler (DC X-2025.06) with the ASAP7 [8] predic-tive 7nm finFET library (RVT, TT corner, 0.7 V/25°C). SRAM area, energy, and leakage are modeled with CACTI [3] at 32 nm and scaled to 7 nm via the polynomial factors of Still-maker and Baas [62]. Cross-check (45 nm) agrees within 11%. Full-model decode latency and system throughput are evaluated using the LIMINAL [14] analytical decode simu-lator. LIMINAL is an externally published framework vali-dated against real-hardware LLM execution to within 7 .6% mean absolute error [14]; it composes independently mod-eled components—Attention weight loading, KV cache ac-cess, MoE FFN execution, TP allreduce, EP all-to-all, and per-MoE-layer kernel launch overhead (symmetrically ap-plied to both baseline and rotor paths). We reproduce its per-primitive cost formulas unchanged and supply only the per-expert MoE FFN cost, itself anchored in measured inputs: NCU-profiled cuBLASLt FP8 GEMMs for the baseline path and the RTL-verified RGU cycle model for the rotor path. The baseline topology uses TP+EP with the minimum GPU count to hold model weights in HBM under a 0 .9× safety mar-gin, leaving headroom for KV cache and activation tensors on the baseline deployment; the FORGE topology replicates seeds across GPUs rather than sharding them. Three context lengths are evaluated: 𝑇 ∈ { 4096 , 32768 , 131072 }.

5.7 Model Quality Validation 

Highlight. Across three model families, FORGE at 𝑟 =0.3 falls short of the same model fine-tuned with LoRA and no rotor replacement. This accuracy cost is what FORGE trades for the system gains of Sections 5.4–5.5. 

FORGE replaces dense expert weights with a structured approximation, so the natural question is whether the result-ing model still behaves well on downstream tasks. To this end, we evaluate model quality across three MoE models (ERNIE-21B, Qwen3-30B, Qwen3-Coder-Next) on WikiText perplexity, HellaSwag accuracy, and ARC-C accuracy. For each model, we show three configurations: the original pre-trained baseline, the same model fine-tuned with LoRA rank-8 (no rotor replacement), and FORGE at 𝑟 =0.3 with the same LoRA setup applied. The results are shown in Table 7. The LoRA-only model is the reference for FORGE, since the two share the same recovery and differ only in rotor replacement. 

Evaluation protocol. All downstream numbers are pro-duced with the EleutherAI Language Model Evaluation Har-ness [23]. Every model is wrapped in the harness’s Hugging-Face backend and scored with the harness’s built-in task defi-nitions. HellaSwag (full validation split) and ARC-Challenge (full test split) are scored zero-shot as a log-likelihood rank-ing over the candidate answers, and we report accuracy as the fraction of examples whose correct answer receives the highest log-likelihood. WikiText-2 perplexity uses the raw test split tokenized with each model’s native tokenizer, cut into 512-token windows with stride 256, and is the expo-nential of the mean token negative log-likelihood. Every reported number is a single, deterministic full pass over the evaluation set, so there is no randomness at evaluation time. FORGE falls short of the LoRA-only model (Table 7). Under an identical rank-8 LoRA budget, the dense model reaches 6.53 perplexity on Qwen3-30B and FORGE reaches 7.65. This is an expected behavior, as rotor replacement is a structured but lossy approximation, so it necessarily consumes part of the quality headroom that LoRA provides. This gap is the explicit quality cost of procedural weight generation, traded for the memory and bandwidth gains. The size of this gap is set by 𝑟 , which a deployer selects against a memory budget rather than a fixed property of the parameterization.

5.8 Limitations 

Our baseline is measured and our design is simulated, so the two sides of every speedup rest on different evidence. The numbers fall into three classes. The FP8 baselines come from cuBLASLt on an H100. The RGU numbers come from its RTL. Generation latency comes from a cycle model ver-ified bit-exact against a golden reference, and area, power, and frequency come from synthesis. The kernel speedups in Table 4 combine the first two through Equation (4). Ev-erything downstream passes through the analytical decode simulator of Section 5.1: the decode latencies in Table 5 and Figure 9, and the capacity and throughput results in Fig-ures 10 and 11. It resolves per-primitive cost and does not capture contention or occupancy effects inside a modified SM. Our synthesis covers the RGU alone. The reported area and power are the unit’s and exclude the cost of wiring it into an SM. They also come from a predictive 7 nm node, while the H100 baseline is built on a commercial 4 nm pro-cess (Section 5.6). We expect the integration cost to be small. FORGE replaces the weight-load path and leaves the rest of the pipeline untouched. The Tensor Core consumes activa-tion and weight tiles exactly as before, and the ISA gains one instruction family parameterized by tile shape. Neither change touches the SIMD execution model. 

## 6 Related Work 

Weight Compression. Post-training quantization [5, 16, 20, 21, 40, 41, 44, 45, 66] and pruning [19, 27, 46, 63] shrink the per-weight payload but still fetch every activated weight from HBM. QuaRot [2] and SpinQuant [42] rotate weights to flatten outliers before quantization, but the rotated tensor is FORGE: Procedural Weight Generation for Sparse MoE Inference ASPLOS ’27, April 11–15, 2027, Heraklion, Greece. 

still materialized and stored. Ecco [7] applies entropy-aware compression to cache-resident data, reducing what crosses the memory hierarchy rather than removing the fetch. We 

synthesize the tile on demand from a compact seed, removing the HBM transfer. 

Structured Parameterization. Low-rank and structured factorizations such as LoRA [17, 29], Monarch [11], butter-fly [13], HashedNets [6], and Fastfood [35] all reduce the effective parameter count, and HyperNetworks [26] gener-ate a larger network’s weights from a smaller one. While these methods reduce parameters, their compressed repre-sentations remain too large for on-chip storage and store the compressed factors in HBM before use. The 𝑂 (log 2 𝑑 )

parametrization of rotors approximates each 𝑂 (𝑑 2) projec-tion [51] and is small enough for the regeneration of the tile directly into on-chip SMEM, bypassing HBM. 

Sparse MoE Systems and Scheduling. Top-𝑘 routing [18, 38, 58] drives frontier MoE designs [4, 9, 15, 24, 32, 54], and systems stacks optimize expert-parallel execution [22, 30, 55] or orchestrate across CPU-GPU memory tiers [31, 33, 59, 60]. All take the per-expert HBM footprint as fixed and schedule around it; FORGE instead eliminates that footprint on replaced layers, collapsing the minimum-fit fleet into a single node and removing cross-node all-to-all from decode. 

LLM Serving and KV Cache Management. Serving frame-works schedule over fixed weight and KV-cache cost [1, 34, 39, 68, 71] or disaggregate prefill from decode [50, 53, 72], while KV-cache interventions shrink the dynamic state via attention sinks, heavy-hitter eviction, and transform cod-ing [61, 67, 69, 70]. These target Attention; FORGE targets the static MoE FFN weights that dominate the remaining decode pressure, and composes with disaggregation by de-ploying the RGU on decode nodes only. 

Near-Data Processing and Transformer Accelerators. 

Processing-in-Memory [28, 36, 37, 49] shortens the weight-movement path without reducing storage; attention acceler-ators [10, 12, 56, 64] and state-space models like Mamba [25] target layers whose volume is dwarfed by MoE FFN weights. FORGE removes the weight data from memory rather than moving computation closer to it, integrating with standard Tensor Core GEMM pipelines [47, 48] without requiring a new memory substrate or a separate accelerator chip. 

## 7 Conclusion 

FORGE replaces HBM weight loading with procedural on-the-fly generation for Sparse MoE FFN layers. The Rotor Generation Unit (fixed-function accelerator within each SM) executes rotor operations in a dedicated pipelined datapath and writes generated weights directly into Shared Memory, bypassing the HBM hierarchy without contending for SIMT resources. This converts the memory-bandwidth-bound MoE FFN decode into a compute-bound operation at ∼0.9% SM area overhead (0.032 𝑚𝑚 2/SM at 7 nm). Across five Sparse MoE models with 30% of the MoE FFN layers replaced, we estimate 1 .3–2 .8× higher system through-put at matched GPU count and 1 .10–1 .15 × lower per-user decode latency at short context, at a cost in downstream ac-curacy relative to the same model fine-tuned without rotor replacement (Section 5.7). Underneath, KV cache capacity grows by 1 .2–2 .6× and the per-expert FP8 kernel speeds up by 2 .3–3 .2× independent of 𝑟 . Higher ratios promise larger system gains; recovery methods that preserve accuracy at those ratios are future work. As MoE expert counts grow, stored weight volume scales linearly while rotor parame-ter seeds scale logarithmically, so the capacity advantage of procedural generation widens with model scale.
