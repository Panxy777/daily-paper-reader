Abstract
Large-scale distributed training increasingly makes collective com-
munication bandwidth-bound. In modern GPU clusters, commu-
nication libraries such as NCCL implement collectives including
AllReduce over heterogeneous interconnects such as NVLink, In-
finiBand, and Ethernet, making efficient use of available network
bandwidth a central systems challenge.
ForestColl showed that collective communication on such fab-
rics can be formulated using fractional tree packing; its scalable
polynomial-time construction is developed for AllGather, with
AllReduce obtained by composing AllGather and ReduceScatter.
We present DualColl, which targets bandwidth-bound AllReduce
directly. Under the tree-based AllReduce model, DualColl applies a
Garg–Könemann-style multiplicative-weights algorithm and, for
any 0 < 𝜖< 1, computes a feasible schedule whose throughput is at
least (1 −𝜖)2 of the optimal AllReduce throughput. Its key compo-
nent is an exact minimum-cost collective-tree oracle that projects
physical-link dual prices onto a logical graph of participating ranks,
reducing each iteration to shortest-path computations followed by
a minimum spanning tree.
We further extend the framework to local equal-next-hop
packet-sprayed routing and to networks without explicit per-tree
bandwidth control. For the latter, we introduce a tree-coupled
max-min bandwidth-sharing model and show that tree duplication
approximates an explicit fractional allocation with a provable
accuracy-complexity tradeoff. Finally, we extend the fractional-
packing framework to additional collective operations and multiple
concurrent collective groups.
Keywords
collective communication, AllReduce, fractional tree packing, multi-
plicative weights, packet spraying, max-min fairness, GPU clusters,
datacenter networks

Introduction
Large-scale distributed training relies heavily on collective commu-
nication. In data-parallel training, for example, workers compute
gradients locally and then use AllReduce to obtain the same ag-
gregated result. Similar communication patterns arise in tensor,
pipeline, and expert parallelism, as well as distributed inference. As
model sizes, accelerator counts, and local computation rates grow,
these collectives increasingly become bandwidth-bound, making
efficient use of network capacity a central performance concern.
In GPU clusters, collective operations are commonly imple-
mented by communication libraries such as NVIDIA NCCL, which
provides topology-aware implementations of AllReduce, AllGather,
ReduceScatter, and related operations across multi-GPU and multi-
node systems [6]. Such systems combine heterogeneous resources:
intra-node links such as NVLink or NVSwitch may be substantially
faster than inter-node Ethernet or InfiniBand links, and oversub-
scription may vary across the network hierarchy. Consequently, ob-
taining high collective throughput requires communication sched-
ules that account for the capacities and topology of the underlying
fabric.
A useful abstraction for bandwidth-bound collective commu-
nication is a collection of communication trees. For AllReduce, a
tree reduces a data chunk toward a root and broadcasts the result
back along the tree. Different chunks may be assigned to different
trees, each operating at a continuous rate. With data partitioned
proportionally to these rates, the resulting steady-state collective
throughput is the sum of the bandwidths assigned to the active
trees.
ForestColl [16] established fractional tree packing as a powerful
approach to collective scheduling on heterogeneous fabrics. In
this formulation, candidate trees receive continuous rates whose
sum is maximized subject to physical-link capacity constraints.
ForestColl’s scalable polynomial-time construction is developed
for AllGather, with AllReduce obtained by composing AllGather
with ReduceScatter. This composition, however, is not guaranteed
to be optimal for AllReduce, as the counterexample in Appendix A
shows.
DualColl instead targets bandwidth-bound AllReduce directly. It
retains the fractional tree-packing abstraction but develops a differ-
ent algorithmic framework for solving it. For the tree-based AllRe-
duce formulation, DualColl computes a feasible solution whose
throughput is within a (1 −𝜖)2 factor of the optimum. To the best
of our knowledge, DualColl is the first polynomial-time algorithm
with a provable near-optimality guarantee for bandwidth-bound
AllReduce on switched topologies.
DualColl uses a Garg–Könemann-style multiplicative-weights
algorithm. The algorithm maintains a dynamic price for every phys-
ical link and repeatedly requests a minimum-cost collective tree.
Its key problem-specific component is an exact oracle that projects
physical-link prices onto a logical graph containing only the partic-
ipating ranks and then computes a minimum spanning tree. This
avoids enumerating the exponentially many candidate trees.
We also extend the framework toward practical network op-
eration. First, we consider local equal-next-hop packet spraying,
in which a logical flow is distributed fractionally over multiple
shortest-path links rather than routed along a scheduler-selected
path. Fine-grained multipath packet spraying is increasingly rele-
vant in AI-oriented network fabrics; for example, the Ultra Ethernet
Transport architecture supports packet spraying across multiple

Second, because real networks do not generally expose explicit

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
• We give, to the best of our knowledge, the first polynomial-
time algorithm with a provable near-optimality guarantee for
bandwidth-bound AllReduce on switched topologies. Unlike
ForestColl’s composition of AllGather and ReduceScatter, which
can be strictly suboptimal for AllReduce (Appendix A), DualColl
targets the AllReduce formulation directly.
• We develop a Garg–Könemann-style approximation algorithm
with an exact minimum-cost collective-tree oracle based on dual-
cost projection and a minimum spanning tree, avoiding explicit
enumeration of exponentially many candidate trees.
• We extend the model to local equal-next-hop packet spraying,
where a logical flow consumes fractional capacity on multiple
physical links.
• We introduce a tree-coupled max-min bandwidth-allocation
model and a tree-duplication construction that approximates
explicit fractional tree bandwidths without requiring direct
per-tree bandwidth control.
• We extend the framework to multiple concurrent collective
groups and other collective operations. In particular, replacing
the AllReduce oracle with one based on rooted spanning arbores-
cences enables AllGather within the same fractional-packing
framework.

its approximation and complexity guarantees. Section 5 consid-

work, and Section 8 concludes the paper.

2.2
Tree-Based AllReduce
In an AllReduce operation, every participating rank initially holds
an input array, and every rank must obtain the element-wise reduc-
tion of all input arrays.
A tree-based AllReduce schedule performs this operation in two
phases. First, data is reduced from the leaves of a rooted logical
tree toward its root. Intermediate ranks combine the values re-
ceived from their children with their own local values. Second, the
root broadcasts the reduced result back to all ranks along the tree.
Note that because data is combined at each intermediate node, the
amount of data that a node sends does not increase as we go higher
in the tree.
A logical tree contains only participating compute nodes.
Switches are not represented as logical vertices; they appear only
in the physical network over which the logical edges are routed.
Let 𝑇be a logical spanning tree over 𝑅. Each logical edge of
𝑇is mapped by the scheduler to a single physical path in 𝐺. The
complete routing of the logical edges determines how much capacity
the tree consumes on every physical edge.
A schedule may use several logical trees in parallel. The input
array is divided into disjoint chunks, and each chunk is assigned to
one tree. Let
T = {𝑇1,𝑇2, . . . ,𝑇𝑘}
be the set of selected trees, and let 𝜆𝑇≥0 denote the bandwidth
assigned to tree 𝑇, per direction, so that the reduce and broadcast
phases each operate simultaneously at rate 𝜆𝑇.
Restricting attention to schedules of this tree form loses no
throughput. Intuitively, consider a tiny shard of the data which
is never split by some optimal schedule. If two ranks both act as
roots for that shard—each independently computing the fully re-
duced value—then work is wasted: the two roots must communicate
their own contributions to one another in any case, so rather than
exchanging raw contributions it is always at least as good for one
of them to send the other its fully reduced result, eliminating one
of the two roots. Iterating this observation collapses every shard to
a single root, after which its reduction and broadcast each form a
tree; a further exchange argument shows the reduce and broadcast
trees may be taken identical. We state the resulting guarantee here
and prove it in Section 3.

3
Optimality of Tree-Based AllReduce
This section proves Theorem 2.1. The model of Section 2 restricts
AllReduce schedules to collections of logical trees, each of which
reduces a shard of the data toward its root and then broadcasts
the reduced shard back along the same tree. We show that this
restriction loses no throughput, so the optimum OPT of the frac-
tional tree-packing formulation is the bandwidth-optimal AllRe-
duce throughput itself, and not merely the optimum over a restricted
class of schedules. Starting from an arbitrary bandwidth-optimal
schedule, we normalize it in three steps, none of which increases
any per-edge load or the completion time: each shard is first re-
duced to a single root (Lemma 3.4), its reduce and broadcast phases
are then made trees rooted there (Lemma 3.5), and finally, under
single-path routing, the two trees are made to coincide (Lemma 3.6).
Lemmas 3.1–3.3 are the tools used along the way.
Setting. We fix a shard: a unit of data that the schedule treats
uniformly, so that the shard is never split into smaller pieces and
every rank’s contribution to it is moved and reduced as a single
whole. A schedule for the shard is a finite set of flows, each carrying
a payload from one rank to another, and each assigned a rate, that
is, a bandwidth. The payload of every flow is the reduction of the
contributions of some subset of the ranks, and the fully reduced
shard is the reduction over all ranks; since reduction is applied
elementwise, every payload has the same size as the shard. A flow
of rate 𝑏places a load of 𝑏times the fraction of its traffic routed
over directed edge 𝑒on 𝑒; the load on 𝑒is the sum of these over all
flows of all shards, and the schedule is feasible if the load on every
directed edge 𝑒is at most 𝐶𝑒. As in Section 2, every physical link
has equal capacity in both directions, 𝐶𝑒= 𝐶¯𝑒, where ¯𝑒denotes the
reverse of directed edge 𝑒.
Flow 𝑔depends on flow 𝑓if the payload of 𝑔is computed from
data delivered by 𝑓; the dependency relation is acyclic. In the
bandwidth-bound model, a flow 𝑔that depends on 𝑓does not wait
for 𝑓to end: it starts as soon as the data of 𝑓begins to arrive and
pipes that data onward as it moves, so dependent flows run con-
currently. We call a rank a root for the shard if it obtains the fully
reduced shard without receiving it, that is, it computes the final
value locally from partial values it has received. At least one root
exists, since the first rank to hold the fully reduced shard cannot
have received it, and every rank ends holding the fully reduced
shard. For a single directed phase 𝑇(a reduce in-tree or a broadcast
out-tree, together with its physical routing) we write 𝑎𝑒(𝑇) for the
load 𝑇places on directed edge 𝑒per unit rate.
Lemma 3.1 (Promotion to the fully reduced shard). Let 𝑓
be a flow whose source already possesses the fully reduced shard. If
the payload of 𝑓is replaced by the fully reduced shard, and the same
replacement is applied to every flow that depends on 𝑓, directly or
transitively, then the resulting schedule is still legal.
Proof. Consider the set D consisting of 𝑓together with all
flows that depend on 𝑓directly or transitively. We promote every
flow in D to carry the fully reduced shard. We must check that each
promoted flow’s payload is actually available at its source when
the flow is sent, so that the schedule remains executable.
Process the flows of D in an order consistent with the depen-
dency relation, which is possible since that relation is acyclic. The
first flow is 𝑓itself, and its source possesses the fully reduced shard
by hypothesis, so it can send it. Consider any later flow 𝑔∈D with
source 𝑢. Every flow of D on which 𝑔depends has been processed
earlier and now delivers the fully reduced shard, so 𝑢is receiv-
ing the fully reduced shard from flows that 𝑔depends on and can
therefore send it. Thus every promoted flow is executable.
A rank that receives a promoted flow receives the fully reduced
shard, and holding the final value it is complete for this shard
regardless of any other partial values, so its state is consistent. A
flow not in D does not depend on 𝑓, so none of its inputs were
altered and its payload is unchanged; hence every rank outside
the recipients of D ends exactly as before, and in particular every
rank that was already complete remains complete. The schedule is
therefore legal.

and does not change the completion time. Suppose the shard has

form a partition of the ranks: if a rank were served by two roots,

Call a flow inter-group if its endpoints lie in different sets of this
partition. At least one inter-group flow exists: each root holds the
fully reduced shard, which incorporates the contributions of ranks
in every group, and those contributions can only leave their group
along an inter-group flow. Since the dependency relation is acyclic
and there are finitely many flows, we may choose a last inter-group
flow 𝑓, one on which no other inter-group flow depends (there may
be several). Say 𝑓goes from a rank of 𝑆𝑖to a rank of 𝑆𝑗, with 𝑖≠𝑗.
Consider the flows that depend on 𝑓, directly or transitively, and
that lead to 𝑟𝑗. If no such chain exists, then 𝑓delivers data that
never reaches 𝑟𝑗and never leaves 𝑆𝑗toward any other group, so
𝑓is useless and may be deleted, decreasing the load and leaving
the set of roots unchanged; we may therefore assume such a chain
exists. Let C be this chain of flows, from 𝑓up to and including the
flow that delivers into 𝑟𝑗.
We now promote C: replace the payload of 𝑓by the fully reduced
shard and apply the same replacement along C. For this to be
executable, the source of 𝑓must hold the fully reduced shard when
𝑓fires. It lies in 𝑆𝑖, so 𝑟𝑖delivers the fully reduced shard to it; we
therefore let 𝑓forward the fully reduced shard as it arrives at its

from completing. By Lemma 3.1 the result is a legal schedule, and
by Lemma 3.3 it is not slower.

4
DualColl’s Algorithm
Section 2 formulated bandwidth-bound AllReduce scheduling as the
fractional tree-packing problem in Equations (1)–(3). Although this
formulation contains only one capacity constraint per physical link,
it has one variable for every feasible collective tree and its physical-
path mapping. The number of such routed trees is exponential
in the number of participating ranks, which prevents their direct
enumeration.
DualColl solves this problem using a Garg–Könemann-style
multiplicative-weights framework. Rather than constructing the
complete linear program, the algorithm maintains a positive price
for every physical edge and repeatedly selects a collective tree
having minimum cost under the current prices. The selected tree
is added to the fractional packing, after which the prices of the
physical edges it uses are increased.
The key problem-specific component of the framework is there-
fore a minimum-cost collective-tree oracle. DualColl implements
this oracle by projecting the current physical-edge prices onto a log-
ical graph over the participating ranks and computing a minimum
spanning tree in the projected graph.

The fractional packing algorithm of Section 4 computes an explicit

it returns a set of logical trees 𝑇1, . . . ,𝑇𝑘and assigns each tree a
continuous bandwidth 𝑎𝑖, with each logical edge mapped to a single
chosen physical path. In that model, the scheduler directly controls
both the route of every flow and the bandwidth of every tree, subject
to the physical capacity constraints.
Real deployments differ from this idealization in two respects.
First, when packet spraying is used, the fabric spreads a logical flow
across several physical paths rather than allowing the scheduler
to choose a path. Second, systems usually do not provide explicit
per-tree bandwidth control; instead, bandwidth is allocated dynami-
cally by the transport protocol and by the network, often according

to a max-min fairness principle. This section addresses both. We
first describe the routing mechanics of the packet-spraying model,
which are independent of how bandwidth is subsequently allocated.
We then define the tree-coupled version of max-min fairness appro-
priate for collective communication, generalize it to packet-sprayed
flows, and finally show that by duplicating trees, the protocol model
can approximate any explicit fractional allocation arbitrarily well.
Packet spraying also forces a change in the formulation itself,
which we flag at the outset. Under the scheduler-routed model
we were entitled to assume that a tree reduces and broadcasts
along the same logical tree: Lemma 3.6 shows that any schedule
with distinct reduce and broadcast trees can be converted, at no
loss of throughput, into one built from such simple trees. That
argument reverses a tree’s routing and charges the reversed phase
the mirrored load on the opposite directed edges, which is valid
precisely because single-path routing sends a reversed logical edge
back along the same physical path, flipping the induced loads 𝑝𝑒(𝐹)
on every physical link. Local equal next-hop spraying does not have
this property. What survives is the weaker structure of Lemmas 3.4
and 3.5, which make no reference to how a logical edge is routed—
they use only the dependency structure among flows and the fact
that the schedule does not split a shard—and therefore hold verbatim
under packet spraying: each shard still has a single root, with a
reduce in-tree and a broadcast out-tree rooted at it, but the two
trees need not coincide. Accordingly, in the packet-sprayed setting
a packing variable is a pair consisting of an in-tree and an out-tree
sharing a root, and the oracle must select the best such pair rather
than a single tree. Section 5.2 develops this formulation and its
oracle.
5.1
Packet-Spraying Routing
We first describe packet spraying purely as a routing mechanism,
independent of how bandwidth is later allocated. Modern datacen-
ter networks often contain several shortest paths between a pair of
endpoints. In packet spraying, traffic between a pair of endpoints
is split across the shortest paths. Since collective communication
transfers large data chunks, we represent the long-run traffic dis-
tribution induced by packet spraying as fractional physical-link
loads.
We consider local equal next-hop packet spraying. At each switch,
a packet is forwarded uniformly at random among all outgoing
next hops that continue along a shortest path to its destination.
The eligible next-hop set is determined by the routing topology
and does not change in response to instantaneous congestion or
Layer-2 PAUSE state. This forwarding rule induces fixed long-run
physical-link fractions for each logical flow, which can therefore
be incorporated directly into the fractional tree-packing model. We
do not consider adaptive packet spraying in which congestion or
flow-control state dynamically changes next-hop eligibility.

5.4
Max-Min Fair Realization
We now turn to how bandwidth is allocated once trees are in-
stantiated. In the packet-sprayed formulation, we treat each re-
duce/broadcast tree pair as one collective object whose constituent

flows share a common rate; all definitions below apply to such pairs
by summing the loads of both phases. We first define tree-coupled
max-min fairness for single-path routing, and then generalize it to
packet-sprayed flows.
In ordinary flow-level max-min fairness, each flow is treated
independently. A collective tree is different: it is not a single physical
flow, but a collection of physical flows corresponding to the logical
edges of the tree. Moreover, the tree can only make progress at the
rate of its slowest flow. If one edge of the tree is bottlenecked, the
whole tree pipeline slows down to that rate.
We therefore use a tree-coupled max-min fairness model. Each
tree 𝑇𝑖consists of a set of physical flows induced by routing its
logical edges over the physical topology. The bandwidth 𝐵(𝑇𝑖) of
the tree is the common rate at which all of its flows operate. If
any flow of 𝑇𝑖becomes bottlenecked, then all other flows of 𝑇𝑖are
clamped to the same rate. This captures the synchronous nature
of a tree-based collective: a tree cannot complete its assigned data
chunk faster than its slowest required communication path.
Assume first that every logical edge is routed over a unique phys-
ical path. The tree-coupled max-min allocation can be described by
the following iterative procedure.
It is important to note that this procedure describes how the
resulting allocation is calculated, not a computation that the net-
work carries out. The bandwidth given to each tree is determined
by the network itself, dynamically, according to its transport and
congestion-control protocol. The network does not run this algo-
rithm; rather, the algorithm is our model for predicting the rates
that the network’s max-min fair sharing will produce, so that the
scheduler can reason about them when choosing which trees to
instantiate. Tree-coupled max-min fairness is thus an idealized an-
alytical model of protocol-determined bandwidth allocation. We do
not claim that any particular congestion-control protocol realizes
exact max-min fairness; rather, max-min fairness is a standard and
analytically tractable approximation of the rates that such protocols
tend to produce, and we use it to reason about the effect of tree
duplication.

5.5
Emulating Explicit Bandwidths
We now show how to emulate an explicit bandwidth allocation
using only max-min fair sharing. The key idea is tree duplication.
If one tree should receive twice as much bandwidth as another, we
instantiate two identical copies of the first tree and one copy of
the second. Under max-min fairness, identical competing copies
receive equal rates; the total bandwidth assigned to the original
tree is the sum of the rates of its copies.
For example, suppose the explicit allocation assigns bandwidth
100 to Tree A and bandwidth 50 to Tree B. If only these two trees are
instantiated, a max-min fair protocol may not produce the desired
ratio. Instead, we instantiate two copies of Tree A and one copy
of Tree B. If the three instantiated trees receive equal bandwidth
50, then the two copies of Tree A together achieve bandwidth
50 + 50 = 100, while Tree B receives bandwidth 50.

The ReduceScatter Operation
In the ReduceScatter operation, every node starts with a full input
vector and ends with a specific reduced block of the data. As in
AllGather, there is a set of trees T𝑗associated with each node 𝑣𝑗;
here, however, the edges in each tree are directed towards the root
𝑣𝑗, representing the flow of partial results being aggregated from
the leaves to the root. The completion time is again governed by the
node whose aggregation is slowest, so the objective is the same max-
min objective as in AllGather, max min𝑗Λ𝑗with Λ𝑗= Í

Why we do not obtain ReduceScatter by reversal. ReduceScatter is
commonly treated as the directional dual of AllGather and obtained
by reversing every logical edge of an AllGather solution; this is
what ForestColl does, and under scheduler-routed single-path rout-
ing it is sound. When every logical edge is mapped to one physical
path, reversing that path preserves feasibility whenever the two
directions of each physical link have equal capacity, since the re-
versed flow consumes on ¯𝑒exactly what the original consumed on

longer applies. The fractions 𝑝𝑒(𝐹) induced by a flow 𝐹= (𝑢, 𝑣)

branching degrees |Γ𝑣(𝑐)| along it. The reversed flow (𝑣,𝑢) is routed

Our Solution. By Lemma 6.1, applied with one class per root,
this max-min objective again has the same optimum as a fractional
packing over 𝑛-tuples of trees. Solving ReduceScatter directly there-
fore requires only one change to the AllGather oracle of Section 6.1.
There, a packing variable is an 𝑛-tuple of trees, one rooted at each
node, and the oracle returns for each root a minimum-weight out-
arborescence, whose edges are directed away from the root, match-
ing the outward flow of a broadcast. For ReduceScatter the data
flows inward instead, so the oracle returns for each root a minimum-
weight in-arborescence, whose edges are directed toward the root;
equivalently, one runs the same minimum-arborescence compu-
tation on the projected graph with its edge orientations reversed.
Everything else is unchanged: the projected weights are computed
from the current dual prices exactly as before, each tuple receives a
common bandwidth, and the surrounding multiplicative-weights
framework, its stopping rule and its scaling step are untouched.
Because the two oracles differ only in the orientation of the arbores-
cences they compute, and an in-arborescence on 𝑛nodes costs the
same as an out-arborescence, ReduceScatter inherits the AllGather
running time and approximation guarantee, and the argument is
valid under packet-sprayed routing as well as under single-path
routing.
6.3
Multiple Concurrent AllReduce-Sets
In complex distributed systems, an AllReduce-set is a specific sub-
set of compute nodes participating in an independent AllReduce
operation. We consider two distinct optimization objectives for this
scenario: maximizing the total aggregate bandwidth across all con-
current sets, or maximizing the bottleneck (slowest) AllReduce-set.
Our Solution. An important assumption required for optimality
in this context is that compute nodes only participate in the routing
and computation of their own AllReduce-sets. Therefore, a spanning
tree for a given set contains only and all the nodes belonging to
that specific set.
To maximize the total aggregate bandwidth across all sets, we
utilize the original fractional packing algorithm (Section 4). During
each oracle iteration, the oracle computes the MST for each active
AllReduce-set independently, and strictly returns the single tree
(from any set) with the absolute minimum weight.
Alternatively, to maximize the slowest AllReduce-set, we apply
Lemma 6.1 with one class per concurrent set: the bottleneck
objective then has the same optimum as a fractional packing whose
columns are 𝑘-tuples of trees, one spanning each AllReduce-set,
all sharing a common rate. To solve this, we structure our oracle
to compute independent spanning trees per set during each
iteration (utilizing only the compute nodes relevant to that
specific set). The single column vector returned to the solver
is exactly the union of these independently optimal spanning
trees. This increases the oracle complexity by a factor of 𝑘, giving

collective-communication algorithms. Classical MPI work de-

AllReduce and ReduceScatter [7, 8, 13], while modern distributed-
training systems commonly use GPU-oriented libraries such as
NVIDIA NCCL [6] and frameworks such as Horovod [10]. Blue-

by decomposing it into parallel ReduceScatter and AllGather oper-

recent systems provide increasingly programmable communication
layers; for example, MSCCLang provides a language for writing
custom GPU collective algorithms together with an optimizing
compiler [3], while MSCCL++ provides low-level communication
primitives and a DSL for constructing high-performance GPU
communication kernels [5]. DualColl is complementary to these
systems: our focus is the algorithmic construction of provably
high-throughput schedules for a given heterogeneous network
fabric.
Topology-aware synthesis and tree packing. Several systems auto-
matically construct collective algorithms for a given topology. SCCL
formulates collective synthesis as an SMT problem and searches for
algorithms along the latency–bandwidth tradeoff [1], while TACCL
uses user-provided communication sketches to guide synthesis on
larger heterogeneous topologies [11]. Blink [15] is particularly re-
lated to DualColl: it applies a multiplicative-weight approximation
algorithm to pack spanning trees on heterogeneous GPU topologies
and uses the resulting structures to implement collectives including
AllReduce. DualColl differs in the optimization problem and guar-
antees considered here: we formulate bandwidth-bound AllReduce
directly, prove that the tree-based restriction loses no through-
put under our full-duplex model, and obtain a polynomial-time
(1 −𝜖)2 approximation guarantee for the resulting AllReduce prob-
lem. Blink’s tree-packing formulation does not directly support
switched topologies.
ForestColl. The work most closely related to ours is Forest-
Coll

which
constructs
throughput-optimal
collective
schedules on heterogeneous network fabrics by packing broadcast
or aggregation spanning trees. Its optimality characterization
is based on the network bottleneck cut and also enables switch

collective group, DualColl additionally addresses packet-sprayed
routing, tree-coupled max-min bandwidth allocation, and multiple
concurrent collective groups.
8
Conclusion
We formulated the scheduling of bandwidth-bound collective
operations as a fractional tree-packing LP under an explicit
bandwidth-allocation model. By combining the Garg–Könemann
algorithm with an exact dual-cost projection oracle, we obtain
a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS) that
computes a solution arbitrarily close to the optimal fractional
tree-packing value on any static datacenter topology satisfying
our network model. To the best of our knowledge, DualColl is the
first polynomial-time algorithm with a provable near-optimality
guarantee for bandwidth-bound AllReduce on switched topologies.
We further extend the framework beyond the scheduler-routed
setting: to local equal next-hop packet spraying, where the reduce
and broadcast trees must be packed as pairs; to networks without
explicit per-tree bandwidth control, where tree duplication realizes
a fractional allocation under tree-coupled max-min fairness; and to
the other collective operations and to multiple concurrent collective
groups.
