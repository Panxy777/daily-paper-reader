Marcos Hernanz 

Escuela Politécnica Superior, Universidad Autónoma de Madrid 

marcos@hernanz.io 

Abstract 

Many open mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models train with Muon instead of AdamW. Most balance the load with a bias on each expert’s routing score, adjusted after every step. Their recipes disagree on whether Muon should also train the router. In models with 55M and 108M active parameters, we measure how the optimizer changes the span of these biases, the largest minus the smallest in a layer. The span grows when all tokens favor the same experts, so it shows how hard a layer is to balance. At learning rates tuned on shorter runs, Muon raised the span of the first MoE layer at every seed, roughly doubling it on average, while the other layers changed much less. The rise came from Muon on the rest of the network, whichever optimizer trained the router. In those runs, the inputs to the first router shared a direction, and soon after the warmup its busiest expert averaged four to seven times its fair share. Over the first 3,000 steps, subtracting the batch mean from these inputs removed this overload while barely changing the loss. On the smaller model, halving Muon’s learning rate removed most of the rise and slightly improved the loss. Moving only the router to AdamW made the loads shift more during the warmup and slightly raised the loss, so at this scale the results favor keeping the router on Muon.

# 1 Introduction 

Most of the leading open language models of 2026 are mixture-of-experts (MoE) models [12, 27, 33, 48, 72]. An MoE layer has many feed-forward networks, the experts, and a router that sends each token to a few of them [18, 29, 56]. Left alone, the router tends to send most tokens to a few experts, which then train on more data and attract still more tokens [56]. Earlier models such as GShard and the Switch Transformer countered this with an auxiliary balance loss that penalizes uneven loads [18, 29, 56]. DeepSeek-V3 switched to auxiliary-loss-free balancing [11, 63], and most recent open models have followed, among them DeepSeek-V4, GLM-5.3, and MiMo-V2.6 [12, 69, 72], some keeping a small balance loss as well. Each expert has a bias that is added to its score when the router selects experts. After every step, the bias of an expert that received more than its fair share of the tokens moves down by a fixed amount, and the bias of an expert that received less moves up. We refer to this balancer as the bias. Most of the leading open MoE models now train their weight matrices with Muon [25, 32] instead of AdamW [35]. Muon orthogonalizes the update of each weight matrix, which sets all the sin-gular values of the update to one. The recipes differ on whether Muon trains the router (Table 1). DeepSeek-V4, Kimi K3, and GLM-5.3 do not exclude it from Muon, while Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, MiMo-V2.6, and IQuest-Q1 do. Only the report of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next gives reasons, among them that Muon made the router unstable early in training with no significant gain later [48]. No report shows data that compare the options, and the studies that compare optimizers in MoE models train without the bias or do not measure the bias itself [28, 32, 36, 37, 59, 61, 64, 70]. The bias pushes the loads toward their fair shares whatever the router prefers, so the loads say little about the router. We measure instead how hard the bias works, by its span, the largest minus the smallest bias in a layer (§3.2). The span stays near zero when the scores of the router spread the tokens evenly on their own, and it grows with any preference for some experts that all tokens share. When the span is large, the bias decides much of the routing, and the loads stay uneven while it catches up with changes in the scores. We study how the optimizer changes the span in two MoE language models trained on FineWeb-Edu [45], a small one with 55M and a large one with 108M active parameters. At learning rates tuned for each optimizer on shorter runs, Muon raised the span of the first MoE layer at every seed, from 0.084 to 0.194 on average (Figure 1), and it did so again at all nine seeds of three later experiments (§4.4). We had expected the opposite. Under Muon the update of an expert has the same size however many tokens the expert receives, which should weaken the feedback in which busy experts become busier. The span of the first MoE layer followed the optimizer of the backbone and changed little with the optimizer of the router (§5.2). With the backbone on Muon, the inputs of the router of the first MoE layer shared a direction, with a mean cosine similarity of about 0.4 against 0.07 under AdamW on the small model. With the backbone on Muon, moving the router to AdamW made the loads shift more during the warmup, on both model sizes and at every learning rate of a stress test, and at the tuned rates it raised the loss slightly at every seed (§5.6). In the large-model experiment, loss spikes occurred only in the runs of Muon with the router on AdamW, although the tuning of the large model had spikes in its run of Muon at twice the selected learning rate. For models of our size, these results favor keeping the router on Muon when the backbone is on Muon, and they point to the first MoE layer as the one most affected by the optimizer. Our models have about 50 to 2,000 times fewer active parameters than those whose recipes motivate the question (§6.3).

3.2 The Span and the Shared Direction 

We call max 𝑖 𝑏 𝑖 − min 𝑖 𝑏 𝑖 the span of a layer. Adding the same constant to every bias leaves the selection unchanged, so only the differences between biases matter, and the span is the largest of them. If the router gives one expert a score 0.1 higher than another for every token, the two receive equal loads only when the bias of the first is about 0.1 below that of the second, so the span is then about 0.1 or more. The span has a ceiling. When 𝑏 𝑖 − 𝑏 𝑗 ≥ 1 , expert 𝑖 ranks above expert 𝑗 for every token, since the scores lie in (0, 1) . Then 𝓁 𝑖 ≥ 𝓁 𝑗 , and Eq. (2) cannot widen the gap. A difference between two biases therefore grows only while it is below 1, by at most 2𝑢 per step, and the span never exceeds 1 + 2𝑢 .Following Wang et al. [65], let 𝐜 be the mean of the router inputs 𝐱 1, … , 𝐱 𝑁 of a batch. The logit of expert 𝑖 for token 𝑛 splits as

This preference grows with the length of the mean input and with the spread of the rows of the router along it.

The early value averages over the 0.05 𝑆 steps after the warmup of a run of 𝑆 steps, which are steps 501 to 1,262 with 1B tokens and 501 to 2,788 with 3B tokens, and the warmup value averages over steps 1 to 500, with the load churn of the first logged step taken as zero. We call the early value of the overload of the first MoE layer the early overload , and the warmup value of the load churn over all MoE layers the warmup churn .

# 4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Models and Data 

The models are decoder-only Transformers [50, 62] with RMSNorm [73], rotary position embed-dings [60], normalized queries and keys [14, 22], gated attention [47], and SwiGLU feed-forward networks [55]. Block 0 has a dense FFN, and every later block has the MoE layer of §3.1 with 

𝐸 = 32 routed experts, of which 𝑘 = 4 are selected. We call the MoE layer of block 1 the first MoE layer and its router the first router . The small model has 8 blocks of width 512 and 55M active parameters out of 303M, and the large model 12 blocks of width 768 and 108M active out of 496M, with the embedding table and the output head counted only in the totals (Table 7 in Appendix A). Both train on sequences of 1,024 tokens in batches of 64, so an expert with its fair share receives 8,192 tokens per step. The bias follows Eq. (2) with 𝑢 = 0.001 , the rate of DeepSeek-V3 and DeepSeek-V4 [11, 12], and keeps this rate until the last step, while DeepSeek-V3 stopped the updates for its last 500B tokens. The bias is the only balancer except where we add a balance loss. The training data are the sample-10BT subset of FineWeb-Edu [45], split into tokens by byte-level byte-pair encoding [50, 54] with a vocabulary of 16,384. A permutation fixed by the seed orders the training sequences. The validation loss is the mean cross-entropy on the same 2.1M held-out tokens in every run. The specialization uses 2M tokens from each of four domains, held-out FineWeb-Edu, OpenWebMath [43], codeparrot-clean-valid [8], and the English Wikipedia [67]. 

4.2 Partitions and Optimizers 

We divide the parameters into six groups, which are the routers, the routed experts, the shared experts, the attention, the dense FFN of block 0, and a last group with the embedding table, the output head, and the gains of the normalization layers. AdamW trains the last group in every run, as in the reports that use Muon [12, 25, 32]. A partition assigns AdamW or Muon to each of the other five groups, and the four groups other than the routers form the backbone. Table 2 lists the partitions we use. The first three, which we call the recipe partitions , are the choices of current recipes. DeepSeek-V3 trains every weight matrix with AdamW [11], DeepSeek-V4 and Moonlight train every weight matrix with Muon except the embedding table, the output head, and a few small ones in DeepSeek-V4 [12, 32], and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next keeps the router on AdamW [48]. We call the second and the third the two Muon partitions . In every partition, each optimizer uses its own tuned rate (§4.3), except where an experiment scales the rates. Muon follows Eq. (4) with 𝜇 = 0.95 , five Newton–Schulz steps, and 𝜆 = 0.1 , as in Moonlight [32]. It treats the router of each layer as one matrix and, as DeepSeek-V4 does, each of the three matrices of every routed expert as a separate matrix. AdamW uses 𝛽 1 = 0.9 , 𝛽 2 = 0.95 , and 𝜆 = 0.1 , as in DeepSeek-V3 and DeepSeek-V4 [11, 12]. The control keeps the momentum of Muon and the size of its update but drops the orthogonalization. It rescales the Nesterov momentum 𝜇𝐌 𝑡 + 𝐆 𝑡 

of each matrix to the Frobenius norm √min(𝑝, 𝑞) of an orthogonal matrix of full rank and then applies the factor and the weight decay of Eq. (4). The updates of all experts therefore have the same size, as under Muon, while the singular values of each update stay as unequal as those of the momentum.

4.3 Learning Rates 

Since the best learning rates of AdamW and Muon differ, we tune the peak rate of each optimizer separately [53, 66], on the partition that trains all five groups with it. The tuning uses the small model, seed 0, and 0.5B tokens, with five rates for AdamW and Muon and six for the control, each twice the one before, and selects the rate with the lowest final validation loss. It selects 2 × 10 −3 

for AdamW, 4 × 10 −3 for Muon, and 5 × 10 −4 for the control, none at the end of its grid, and the losses of Muon at 2 × 10 −3 and 4 × 10 −3 differ by 0.003. AdamW is tuned first, and in every run of Muon or of the control, AdamW trains the embedding table, the output head, and the gains at the rate tuned for AdamW. The small model uses these rates with 1B tokens, twice the tuning length. The large model is tuned again with 1B tokens at 0.125 to 1 times the rates of the small model, and this tuning selects 10 −3 for AdamW and 2 × 10 −3 for Muon. The large model then trains on 3B tokens, so we multiply both rates by 0.7, about 3−0.32 , following the fall of the best rate with the number of tokens that Bjorck et al. [6] fit for AdamW on models of 760M parameters or more and Ren et al. [51] for Muon constrained to a hypersphere at 208M parameters. The rate of each optimizer rises linearly over 500 steps and then decays along a cosine [34] to a tenth of its peak, and each optimizer clips the gradient of its parameters at norm 1.0 (Appendix A).

The fast bias halved the rise, from 0.075 to 0.040 (Table 5), and cut the excess early overload from 2.84 to 0.54, but the span stayed higher under Muon at every seed. For a softmax router trained only to balance its loads, with small logits, Wang et al. Our balance loss lowered the score offset of all three recipe partitions and the early overload of both Muon partitions at every seed, as this predicts. # 6 Discussion and Limitations 

6.1 An Account of the Rise 

Our results support the following account. With the backbone on Muon, the inputs of the first router share a direction, and by Eq. (3) their mean gives every token the same preference among the experts, in proportion to how much the rows of the router differ along it. Each bias moves by 𝑢 per step, so the favored experts are overloaded until the biases catch up. The span followed the score offset across the arms, and in the planned comparisons that crossed the optimizers of the router and the backbone, the span and the shared direction both followed the backbone. In the 3,000-step probes, centering the input of the first router removed the early overload on both model sizes and changed the loss by less than 0.003. Three questions remain open. First, we do not know what spreads the rows of the router along the shared direction. On the small model, half the tuned rate of Muon and the fast bias each lowered this spread and left the length of the mean input almost unchanged, and in both cases the early overload stayed below twice the fair share. This suggests that the rows move apart because the loads are uneven, since a row receives gradient only from the tokens routed to its expert, but we did not test this. Second, we do not know how the routed experts alone on Muon gave the largest span of the ablation at an input cosine of 0.146. The control, which gives every expert an update of the same size without the orthogonalization, had a higher span than AdamW at every rate above its tuned rate (Appendix C.1), so updates of equal size may matter, and training only the routed experts with the control would test this. Third, and most important, we do not know why, of the partitions we trained, only the whole backbone on Muon raised the input cosine of the first router to about 0.4. With only the attention and the dense FFN on Muon, every weight matrix that computes the input of this router except the embedding table was on Muon, yet the input cosine stayed at 0.054 while the early overload reached 5.07. The shared direction therefore seems to need the routed or the shared experts on Muon, although the experts shape this input only through the gradients they send back. The routed experts alone were not enough, and no partition other than the whole backbone put the shared experts on Muon. 

6.2 The Router and the First MoE Layer 

At our scale, the comparisons favor the router on Muon when the backbone is on Muon, as do the runs of Lau and Su [28] without a balancing bias, while Qwen3.8-Flash-Next keeps its router on AdamW because, according to its report, Muon made it unstable early in training. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next is far larger, with 125B parameters of which 6B are active, and its own stress test used a model with 25B parameters. Its report shows no data on the router and states neither the balancer nor the loads, so we cannot tell whether the early instability it describes is the warmup churn we measure. The released configuration [49] lists a balance loss and no bias, so its router may face a different balancer than ours. With the backbone on AdamW, moving the router to Muon, which no recipe we cite does, changed the span of the first MoE layer by +0.004 and the loss by −0.005 

on average, with signs that varied over the seeds. In the stress test, the largest absolute router logit under Muon at 16 times the tuned rates averaged 98 over the seeds and reached 170 at one seed, while that of a router on AdamW stayed below 36 (Appendix C.2). Our sigmoid router has no softmax and runs in single precision, and the stress test had too few loss spikes to tell whether such logits harm training, but in a softmax router computed in bfloat16 they could cause the roundoff errors that the router z-loss reduces [74].   

> 20 Muon Makes the First MoE Layer Harder to Balance 6Discussion and Limitations

In our models, which have no capacity limit, the overload of the first MoE layer had no cost that we could measure. The only possible exception is the loss spikes with the backbone on Muon. At the tuned rates they came only on the large model with the router on AdamW, and otherwise only at 2 to 16 times the selected rates, in the tunings and the stress test. At every one of them except a spike with the router on AdamW in the stress test, the span of that layer was 0.99 or more, near its ceiling (Appendices C.2 and C.4). When experts sit on separate devices, busy experts slow down training [29], and with a capacity limit the tokens beyond it are dropped [18], so four to seven times the fair share for hundreds of steps would cost time or tokens. Our models, like DeepSeekMoE and IQuest-Q1 [10, 23], have a dense block before the first MoE layer, and under Muon the MoE layer after it was still the hardest to balance. Centering removed the overload, but we tested it only over 3,000 steps and in one layer, and the batch mean makes the routing of a token depend on its batch, so a practical version would subtract a running mean. The fast bias lowered the overload, but in the probe of the large model, at one seed, the loads of the first MoE layer still shifted about three times as much as under AdamW (Appendix C.4). 

6.3 Limitations 

Our models have 55M and 108M active parameters, about 50 to 2,000 times fewer than the models whose recipes motivate our question, and they train on at most 3B tokens, about four orders of magnitude fewer. In trained Moonlight and DeepSeek-V2-Lite, which have almost the same architecture but differ in optimizer, balancer, router scoring function, tokenizer, and training data, the mean pairwise cosine similarity of the hidden states is higher in Moonlight, trained with Muon, in the lowest 20 of the 26 layers plotted and lower in the last three [65, Fig. 15], so at scale a stronger shared direction under Muon may persist mainly in the lower layers. The input cosine and the early overload of the first MoE layer were higher on the large model, which trained on three times as many tokens as the small one, and we cannot say whether the overload of this layer would settle in a much longer run. In Nemotron 3 Ultra, trained with AdamW, the busiest expert of the first MoE layer reached about 12 times its fair share as its training grew unstable, so in large models this layer may be hard to balance under either optimizer, at least in unstable runs (§2). We train one architecture on one dataset, with 32 routed experts of which a sigmoid router selects 4, a shared expert, one dense block before the first MoE layer, and no capacity limit. The experts of the large model are narrower than its width, 512 against 768, while those of the small model are wider, 768 against 512, so the two sizes differ in more than scale. The sigmoid bounds the scores and gives the span its ceiling, which the span of the first MoE layer nearly reached at some steps on both models, whereas the scores of DeepSeek-V4 have no upper bound [12]. We test one balancer, the bias, at two update rates, and add a balance loss at one weight. We use one batch size, 65,536 tokens, which sets the noise in the loads that the bias reacts to, and one warmup of 500 steps, inside which the shared direction appeared. We evaluate the models only by their validation loss, which in small models trained on few tokens may not tell routing methods apart [17]. The rates of the small model were tuned with one seed and 0.5B tokens and used with 1B, where half the tuned rate of Muon trained better and removed most of the rise. We did not retune AdamW on the small model at 1B tokens or train the large model with 3B tokens at a lower rate of Muon. Bjorck et al. [6] find a steeper fall of the best rate with the number of tokens in their models of 50M and 125M parameters than in larger ones, and ours have 55M and 108M active parameters, so the rates of the large model may be above the best for both optimizers. Each comparison of an experiment rests on three seeds, with no correction for the number of comparisons, and several results of the probes rest on one seed. The probes cover only the first 3,000 steps and, like most of the other exploratory analyses, were chosen after seeing the runs.   

> 21 Muon Makes the First MoE Layer Harder to Balance 7Conclusion

# 7 Conclusion 

In MoE language models with 55M and 108M active parameters, balanced by the auxiliary-loss-free bias, Muon raised the span of the first MoE layer to about twice that of AdamW on average, at learning rates tuned on shorter runs. This span rose with Muon on the backbone, whatever optimizer trained the router, and the backbone on Muon gave the inputs of the first router a shared direction. On the small model, most of the rise disappeared at half the tuned rate of Muon, which trained better at 1B tokens. Centering the input of the first router removed the early overload in 3,000-step probes and changed the loss by less than 0.003. With the backbone on Muon, moving the router to AdamW shifted the loads more during the warmup and, at the tuned rates, raised the loss slightly. The next questions are whether these effects grow or settle at production scale and whether a running mean can replace the batch mean in centering.

C.1 Tuning 

The final validation losses at the tuned rates were 3.386 for AdamW, 3.300 for Muon, and 3.396 for the control, and AdamW with 𝛽 2 = 0.98 , a check of its second-moment decay, reached 3.389. At its tuned rate the control had a span of 0.107 averaged over the layers, below the span of AdamW at each of the five rates of its grid. At every higher rate, the span of the control exceeded all five. At 10 −3 , for example, the control reached a loss of 3.405, close to that of AdamW at its tuned rate, with a span of 0.211. Muon at 1.6 × 10 −2 and 3.2 × 10 −2 , four and eight times the selected rate, had 42 and 37 loss spikes (defined in Appendix C.2) in 20 and 21 episodes of consecutive spikes at steps 1,096 to 3,513, each with the span of the first MoE layer at 0.992 to 1.001 at the nearest logged step, and no other run of the tuning of the small model had one. 

C.2 Stress Test 

Figure 3 shows the runs of the stress test. The losses of the two Muon partitions rose more than that of AdamW above the tuned rates. Their learning-rate sensitivity, the mean over the multiples 1 to 8 of the final loss minus the lowest of these final losses, which follows Wortsman et al. [68] over a narrower range of rates, was 0.24 against 0.18 for AdamW. Up to four times the tuned rates, the largest absolute entries of the residual stream under the Muon partitions were two to three times those of AdamW, in line with the larger activations under Muon that the report of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next found at twice the best rate [48]. From eight times, those of AdamW were larger, so the largest activations did not rank the partitions as the loss did. The largest absolute router logit stayed below 36 whenever AdamW trained the router, and under Muon its mean over the seeds grew from 16 at the tuned rates to 98 at 16 times, with 170 at one seed. A step is a spike when its training loss exceeds the median of the 201 steps centered on it by more than 0.1, the window length and threshold of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next [48], and by more than five robust standard deviations of that difference [26]. Under this rule, the four experiments on the small model with 1B tokens had one spike, in a run of AdamW in the main comparison. The 45 runs at the five multiples had seven episodes of consecutive spikes. Six were at 16 times the tuned rates, three under Muon, two under AdamW, and one with the router on AdamW, and the seventh was under AdamW at twice the tuned rates. The three under Muon were all at one seed, each with the span of the first MoE layer within 2𝑢 of its ceiling, and at the spike with the router on AdamW this span was 0.946. With the fast bias at four times the tuned rates, Muon reached the same loss as with the usual bias and a lower warmup churn.
