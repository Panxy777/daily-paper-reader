# Efficient CPU-GPU collaborative MoE inference via Adaptive Expert Assignment 

## Wenhui Lv, Yunming Liao, Hongli Xu, Yang Xu, Junhao Cheng 

## Abstract 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models reduce inference computa-tion through sparse expert activation, but their large expert memory footprint often exceeds GPU capacity, making ex-pert offloading necessary for memory-constrained inference. However, existing systems typically handle GPU-missed ex-perts by either transferring them to the GPU, incurring sub-stantial PCIe transfer latency, or executing them on the CPU, making the slower CPU computation the bottleneck. To ad-dress these limitations, we present Apex , a CPU-GPU collab-orative MoE inference system that adaptively assigns these missed experts to the CPU or GPU for efficient execution. By exploiting differences in expert token-loads, Apex selec-tively transfers high-load experts to the GPU while execut-ing low-load experts on the CPU. To minimize inference la-tency, Apex adaptively adjusts expert assignments to balance the CPU computation path against the GPU transfer-and-computation path, allowing both execution paths to complete as early as possible. Extensive experiments on three repre-sentative MoE LLMs show that Apex improves prefill speed by up to 51.9% and decode throughput by up to 39.5% over the strongest baseline across the evaluated batch sizes, with consistent gains under different GPU-memory budgets. 

## 1 Introduction 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) [8, 12, 18, 28, 39] architectures have emerged as an effective way to scale the capacity of Large Language Models (LLMs) [3, 27, 38, 40, 46] without pro-portionally increasing inference computation. Instead of ap-plying the same dense feed-forward network to every token, an MoE module contains many specialized expert networks and routes each token to only a small subset of them [4]. De-spite this computational advantage, MoE models still impose a substantial memory footprint [36, 42]. Sparse activation cannot reduce storage requirements, as all expert parameters must remain accessible during inference [32]. Consequently, storing the entire model in limited and expensive GPU mem-ory poses difficulties for private and on-premises deploy-ment [6, 25, 26, 34]. Expert offloading [17, 35, 48] provides a practical solution by storing expert weights in high-capacity CPU host memory. In this context, some activated experts are inevitably absent from GPU memory at runtime, referred to as GPU-missed experts . A fundamental question for of-floaded MoE inference is therefore how to process these missed experts. One option is to execute GPU-missed experts on the GPU ,where the missed experts must first be transferred from host memory over PCI Express (PCIe) before execution. However, GPU misses occur frequently, and each miss incurs an expen-sive expert transfer that is much slower than GPU execution. Even with half of the experts retained on the GPU, over 50% of the required experts frequently miss GPU residency [9, 43]. Prior work [36] shows that on-demand expert transfers slow inference by more than 17 ×. Existing systems commonly employ speculative prefetching [9] to hide part of this cost. They predict the experts required by the next layer from the current layer’s hidden states and transfer the predicted experts in parallel with ongoing computation. However, the available overlap is bounded by approximately one Trans-former layer of computation. Our preliminary experiments in Sec. 2.3 show that transferring the required experts takes at least 5.2 × as long as GPU computation, leaving most of the transfer latency exposed even with prefetching. Pure GPU execution therefore remains transfer-bound. The other option is to execute GPU-missed experts on the CPU . Instead of transferring the expert weights, the GPU sends the much smaller expert inputs to the CPU, which computes the missed experts using weights already in host memory and returns their outputs to the GPU. Meanwhile, the GPU computes its resident experts and then merges the outputs from both devices. This approach transfers only the much smaller inputs and outputs rather than entire ex-perts. However, CPUs provide far lower expert-computation throughput than GPUs, commonly delivering more than 10 ×

lower bfloat16 compute throughput [20, 24, 29]. The GPU must wait for CPU results at each layer, placing CPU exe-cution on the critical path. This bottleneck becomes more severe at the larger batch sizes as the CPU processes a grow-ing expert workload. Existing systems attempt to reduce GPU misses and thereby limit the workload assigned to the CPU. For example, they profile expert popularity offline and keep globally popular experts resident in GPU memory [19], or transfer GPU-missed experts after each inference step to improve subsequent GPU hit rates [15]. Nevertheless, GPU memory remains limited, and the activated experts vary across inference requests, so the CPU inevitably processes part of the expert workload. Its much longer execution time (e.g. , more than 5 .4× the GPU time in our preliminary study in Sec. 2.3) continues to slow down overall inference latency. We argue that the key challenge lies in deciding where to execute GPU-missed experts. Executing them on the GPU incurs transfer latency, whereas executing them on the CPU incurs computation latency. To this end, we propose Apex ,an efficient CPU-GPU collaborative MoE inference system

However, realizing this adaptive assignment poses a sig-nificant challenge. Under CPU-GPU collaborative execution, overall latency is determined by the slower of two concur-rent paths. The CPU path consists of computing the experts assigned to the CPU, whereas the GPU path includes both transferring selected experts and executing all GPU-assigned experts. The number of transferred experts directly shapes both paths. Transferring too many experts exposes exces-sive transfer latency and makes the GPU path the bottle-neck. Transferring too few leaves the CPU with excessive computation, making the CPU path the bottleneck. An effec-tive assignment must therefore reduce enough CPU work to shorten the CPU path without introducing excessive trans-fer overhead on the GPU path. The goal is to minimize the maximum latency of the two concurrent paths. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: 

• We propose Apex , a CPU-GPU collaborative MoE in-ference system that adaptively assigns GPU-missed experts between CPU and GPU execution to balance the two paths and reduce inference latency. 

• To minimize inference latency, Apex combines specu-lative and exact assignment. Speculative assignment predicts high-load experts one layer ahead and starts their transfers early, extending the transfer window. Exact assignment uses an online, rate-driven algo-rithm to determine the remaining CPU/GPU assign-ments that minimize the layer latency. 

• We implement Apex and evaluate it on three repre-sentative MoE models across different batch sizes and GPU memory budgets. Compared with the strongest baselines, Apex improves prefill speed by up to 51.9% and decode throughput by up to 39.5%, with average gains of 31.2% and 31.1%, respectively.

A dense Transformer applies one FFN to every token, whereas an MoE Transformer replaces it with a Mixture-of-Experts mod-ule containing 𝐸 smaller FFNs, referred to as experts [18, 33]. During inference, a router selects only the top-𝐾 experts for each token, where 𝐾 ≪ 𝐸 , and combines their outputs using the corresponding routing scores [7, 23]. 2.2 Execution with Expert Offloading 

Despite the computational efficiency, the large number of ex-pert parameters imposes substantial memory requirements. Expert offloading addresses this issue by keeping the non-expert modules and only a limited subset of experts in GPU memory while keeping all experts in CPU host memory. When the router selects an expert that is absent from the GPU, existing systems generally follow one of two execu-tion paradigms: (1) pure GPU execution transfers every GPU-missed expert to the GPU and performs expert computation, whereas (2) CPU-GPU collaborative execution computes GPU-missed experts in place on the CPU while the GPU processes its resident experts. 

Pure GPU execution. Pure GPU execution performs all expert computation on the GPU. An activated expert already resident in GPU memory can execute immediately, whereas the weights of a GPU-missed expert must traverse PCIe before execution. Existing systems use speculative prefetch-ing to overlap transfers with ongoing computation and re-duce the exposed transfer latency. For example, Mixtral-Offloading [9] estimates the experts required by the next layer from the current hidden states. For token 𝑡 , it feeds hidden state h𝑖,𝑡 to the router of layer 𝑖 + 1 to obtain the predicted routing scores and expert set: 

br𝑖 +1,𝑡 = softmax  Router 𝑖 +1 (h𝑖,𝑡 ) ,

bT𝑖 +1,𝑡 = TopK  br𝑖 +1,𝑡 , 𝐾  . (6) Although h𝑖,𝑡 is not the exact input to the next-layer router, residual connections keep the hidden states of adjacent layers highly similar [9, 22]. The predicted expert set thus provides an early estimate of the next layer’s routing result and can achieve considerable prediction accuracy ( e.g. , over 80% [48]). The system starts transferring these predicted experts while the current layer is still executing. Speculative prefetching can hide only the transfers that fit within the computation window, which spans from the current layer’s MoE module to the next layer’s MoE module. Upon reaching the next layer’s MoE module, any required expert that remains absent from the GPU must be transferred on demand. GPU expert computation is then blocked until the corresponding experts arrive, directly exposing PCIe latency on the GPU execution path. Pure GPU execution therefore trades high GPU compute throughput for waiting on expert transfer, as illustrated in Figure 2(a). 

CPU-GPU collaborative execution. CPU-GPU collabo-rative execution avoids transferring GPU-missed experts by evaluating them directly on the CPU. To reduce CPU com-putation overhead, existing works attempt to decrease the number of GPU misses and thereby limit the expert workload assigned to the CPU. A representative system, Fiddler [19], first profiles expert popularity on a calibration dataset and keeps the most frequently activated experts resident in GPU memory. 2.3 Motivation for System Design 

To quantify the limitations of existing execution paradigms, we conduct preliminary experiments using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B [31] on the MMLU [13] dataset. This model contains 256 experts in each MoE module and activates eight experts for every token. We combine multiple MMLU requests into batches and evaluate the resulting token-loads, transfer la-tency, and CPU/GPU execution latency. These experiments yield the following observations. 

Batched routing activates more experts with highly uneven token-loads. As shown in Figure 3(a), increasing the batch size from 8 to 16 and 24 expands the number of activated experts from 13 to 18 and 27, respectively. A larger activated set creates more potential GPU misses under a fixed GPU-memory budget. More importantly, token assignments remain highly uneven within each batch. At batch size 24, for example, the token-loads of the 27 activated experts range from one token to 23 tokens. Consequently, these experts impose substantially different computation workloads. 

Expert transfer dominates GPU computation. Under a fixed GPU-memory budget, larger activated sets generally create more GPU misses and hence more transfers under pure GPU execution. Figure 3(b) shows that, at batch size 1, expert transfer takes 3.80 ms per layer, compared with only 0.73 ms for GPU expert computation. At batch size 32, expert transfer increases to 20.86 ms while GPU computation takes 1.59 ms, widening the ratio to 13 .1×. Because specula-tive prefetching provides only a limited overlap window, a substantial fraction of this transfer remains exposed. Trans-ferring every GPU-missed expert consequently makes pure GPU execution transfer-bound. 

Collaborative execution leaves inference CPU-bound. 

Following Fiddler [19], we profile expert popularity on the MMLU training split, keep the most popular experts resi-dent on the GPU, and evaluate inference on the validation split at batch size 16. We vary the number of GPU-resident experts from 32 to 96. As shown in Figure 3(c), increasing the GPU-memory budget moves more computation to the GPU and gradually reduces CPU computation. With 32 ex-perts resident on the GPU, the CPU still processes 74.80% of the assignments and takes 3.66 ms per layer, compared with 0.45 ms on the GPU. Even with 96 GPU-resident experts, the CPU receives 43.73% of the assignments and takes 2.98 ms, whereas GPU computation takes only 0.49 ms. Thus, CPU expert execution remains the long-tail latency bottleneck. These observations expose a common limitation of exist-ing approaches: they treat all GPU-missed experts uniformly. Pure GPU execution transfers every missed expert, whereas traditional CPU-GPU collaborative execution computes ev-ery missed expert on the CPU. However, experts have identi-cal transfer sizes while their token-loads differ substantially. Transferring a high-load expert can eliminate considerable CPU computation, whereas transferring a low-load expert incurs the same PCIe cost for little benefit. This insight mo-tivates load-aware execution assignment: high-load experts should be transferred to the GPU, while low-load experts should remain on the CPU.

To provide greater flexibility for adaptive assignment, Apex predicts high-load experts for the next layer and begins transferring during the current-layer. This speculative transfer creates an overlap window in which additional GPU-missed experts can be transferred without exposing transfer latency, allowing more CPU work to be reassigned to the GPU. 

## 3 System Design 

3.1 Overview of Apex 

Apex is an efficient MoE inference system with expert of-floading, where all expert weights are stored in CPU host 

Figure 4. System overview of Apex .memory, while GPU memory holds the non-expert param-eters and a fixed-capacity expert pool. Figure 4 illustrates how Apex coordinates expert execution and transfers along the inference path. At each Transformer layer, inference pro-ceeds from attention execution on GPU to the MoE block, which consists of router computation and CPU-GPU col-laborative expert execution. After attention completes, its output is passed to the router, which determines the exact expert demand and sends it to the transfer controller . Using the current GPU pool state and observed runtime rates, the controller performs exact assignment : it decides which GPU-missed experts should execute on CPU and which should be transferred to GPU, forming an expert execution plan and starting the exact expert transfers. The inference engine fol-lows this plan to group input tokens by expert and dispatch them to the assigned devices. CPU-assigned experts process activations transferred from GPU, while GPU-assigned ex-perts execute after the exact expert transfers complete. The engine then returns CPU results to GPU and merges the expert outputs before proceeding to the next layer. Along-side this main inference path, the attention output of layer 

𝑖 is also passed to the speculative ranker , which applies the next layer’s router to estimate expert demand for layer 𝑖 + 1. The ranker sends this prediction to the transfer controller for speculative assignment . After current-layer exact trans-fers complete, the controller prefetches predicted experts, assigning some next-layer missed experts to GPU in advance while overlapping their transfers with ongoing computation. Throughout this process, the expert pool manager maintains the GPU expert pool, selecting an eviction victim whenever an incoming expert requires a slot and reporting the updated pool state to the controller. The inference engine reports observed expert token-loads to the pool manager, which up-dates token-load-based priorities for every expert to make future eviction decisions.

3.3 Transfer Controller 

The transfer controller combines exact expert assignment for the current layer with speculative expert assignment for the next layer. Together, they adapt CPU-GPU expert assignment to observed and predicted demand: exact assignment bal-ances the current execution paths, while speculative assign-ment uses the available overlap window to place next-layer experts on GPU in advance. Exact transfers take priority over speculative transfers.

3.5 Expert Pool Manager 

The expert pool manager maintains the fixed-capacity expert pool in GPU memory. Before every assignment decision, it reports the latest resident set R𝑖 to the transfer controller. Whenever the transfer controller brings in a new expert, the manager evicts an existing expert according to the eviction policy. The eviction policy aims to remove an expert that is unlikely to be activated again, thereby reducing the probabil-ity of reloading an expert shortly after its eviction. To capture both the recency and frequency of recent routing demand, the manager records an exponentially decayed token-load utility. At inference step 𝑠 , it updates the utility as:

## 4 Performance Evaluation 

4.1 Experimental Setup Implementation. We implement Apex on top of KTrans-formers [5], an efficient CPU-GPU collaborative inference framework. KTransformers keeps the complete expert weights in CPU host memory and retains only a subset of experts in GPU memory. It uses SGLang [47] for GPU execution and provides optimized MoE kernels for CPU expert com-putation. To support Apex , we extend the framework with next-layer expert prediction and prefetching, online CPU-GPU expert assignment, and GPU expert-pool management. We conduct all experiments on a server equipped with one NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48 GB of memory, one Intel Xeon Platinum 8358P CPU, and 512 GB of host memory. The CPU and GPU communicate through PCIe 4.0 ×16, which provides a theoretical peak bandwidth of 32 GB/s. 

Models and workloads. We evaluate Apex using three representative MoE models: Qwen3.5-35B-A3B [31], Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct [30], and GLM-4.5-Air [37]. As sum-marized in Table 1, these models differ substantially in total parameter count, activated parameters, number of total ex-perts, and number of activated experts. They therefore allow us to evaluate Apex across diverse model scales and expert-activation patterns. All models use BF16 precision. None of them can be fully accommodated by the 48 GB GPU, mak-ing expert offloading necessary. We construct the inference workloads from the validation split of MMLU [13] and vary the batch size to evaluate the systems under different serving loads. We use the disjoint training split only for the offline profiling such as collecting expert activation frequencies. 

Baselines. We compare Apex against three representative expert-offloading approaches. 

Mixtral-Offloading [9] follows pure GPU execution and performs all expert computation on GPU. It predicts the experts required by the next layer from the current layer’s intermediate representations and prefetches their weights to reduce exposed transfer latency. 

Fiddler [19] represents static CPU-GPU collaborative exe-cution. It profiles expert activation frequencies offline, places the most frequently activated experts on GPU, and executes every GPU-missed expert directly on CPU. Its expert place-ment remains unchanged during inference, and no experts are transferred at runtime. 

Post-Fetch [15] also executes every GPU-missed expert on CPU. After each inference iteration, however, it transfers the missed experts to GPU for possible reuse in subsequent decoding iterations. To isolate the effects of their transfer and execution poli-cies, we reproduce all baselines within the same KTransform-ers framework and assign them the same GPU expert-pool capacity as Apex .

Metrics. We quantify prefill performance using time to first token (TTFT) and prefill speed, and decode performance using time per output token (TPOT) and decode through-put [1, 49]. TTFT measures the latency until the first output token is generated, while prefill speed measures the num-ber of prompt tokens processed per second. TPOT measures the average time to generate each subsequent output to-ken, while decode throughput measures the total number of output tokens generated per second across the batch. Lower TTFT and TPOT and higher prefill speed and decode through-put indicate better performance.

## 5 Related Work 

The large expert memory footprint of MoE models makes inference challenging under limited GPU memory. Expert offloading alleviates this constraint by storing expert weights in host memory and retaining only a subset on the GPU. Existing approaches primarily differ in how they execute activated experts that are absent from GPU memory. 

Pure GPU execution. Early frameworks, for example, DeepSpeed-Inference [2], use host memory as a backing store and stage model parameters to GPU on demand. MoE-specific systems exploit sparse activation to transfer only the experts selected by the router. Mixtral-Offloading [9] com-bines expert caching with prefetching, which applies a future-layer router to an earlier hidden state and transfers pre-dicted experts before their exact activation is known. MoE-Infinity [42] instead records request-level expert-activation traces and uses the results to guide expert caching and prefetch-ing. Subsequent work improves the timeliness or accuracy of expert prediction. Pre-gated MoE [17] modifies the model so that each gating function selects experts for a later MoE block. ProMoE [35] introduces a learned predictor and coordi-nates prediction, prefetching, and cache replacement to move likely experts into GPU memory before they are requested. fMoE [45] further combines fine-grained expert-selection patterns with semantic information from the input to guide caching and prefetching. Despite these differences, expert computation remains on the GPU, and any GPU-missed ex-pert must be transferred over PCIe before execution. 

CPU-GPU collaborative execution. Other systems ex-ploit CPU computation to reduce expert weight transfers. Instead of transferring GPU-missed experts, they send the much smaller activations to CPU, execute the correspond-ing experts in host memory, and return their outputs to GPU. Fiddler [19] demonstrates that this strategy can out-perform weight transfers for memory-constrained, small-batch MoE inference. It profiles expert popularity to keep frequently activated experts resident on GPU. During in-ference, GPU-missed experts execute on CPU. KTransform-ers [5] provides optimized heterogeneous kernels for exe-cuting selected model operators, including MoE experts, on CPU. Recent work further refines expert residency for collab-orative execution. Huang et al. [15], referred to as Post-Fetch 

in our evaluation, execute a GPU-missed expert on CPU for the current inference step and asynchronously transfer its weights into the GPU for potential reuse by subsequent inference steps. These approaches demonstrate that CPU computation can reduce costly expert transfers and enable both processors to contribute to MoE inference. 

## 6 Conclusion 

This paper presents Apex , a CPU-GPU collaborative infer-ence system for memory-constrained MoE inference. Apex 

uses the token-load of each expert and online measurements of CPU computation, GPU computation, and expert trans-fer to determine its execution device at runtime, avoiding both long CPU execution time and excessive transfer time. We implement Apex on KTransformers and evaluate it on modern MoE models. Compared with the strongest baseline, Apex improves prefill speed by 31.2% on average and up to 51.9%, while increasing decode throughput by 31.1% on average and up to 39.5%. Its advantage persists across all evaluated GPU capacities. These results demonstrate that balancing expert transfer against CPU computation at run-time enables efficient inference of large MoE models under limited GPU-memory budgets.
