# Low-Latency MoE Inference in Space Data Centers: A Wireless-Power-Transfer Perspective 

Haotian Zheng, Zhanwei Wang, and Kaibin Huang, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Space data centers (SpaceDCs) are emerging as platforms for large language models (LLMs), supported by continuous solar-energy harvesting and high-speed intercon-nections. The mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture supports distributed LLM inference by deploying experts across satellites. However, skewed expert activation creates uneven computation power demands despite uniform solar-power supply at the dawn– dusk sun-synchronous orbit (SSO). Consequently, active experts remain power-constrained while harvested power at idle experts is underused. These reduce inference efficiency and increase latency. To address this supply–demand mismatch, we propose the SpaceWPT framework, which uses inter-satellite laser-based wireless power transfer (WPT) to redistribute harvested power. The key design challenge is to balance the computation demands of power-donating and power-receiving satellites under WPT losses: transferring more power accelerates computation at the receiver but leaves less power for the donor. After deriving design principles that simplify WPT control, we characterize the resulting latency tradeoff through a tractable surrogate of average inference latency. Minimizing the surrogate yields a novel two-sided water-filling strategy that determines whether each active expert donates, retains, or receives power according to the water level and its WPT link efficiency. Simulations with Mixtral-8x7B and Phi-3.5-MoE show up to 29.25% lower inference latency than without WPT, with larger gains under more compact formations and higher transfer efficiencies. 

Index Terms —Space data centers, Wireless Power Transfer, Mixture of Experts, Power Control. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The sixth-generation (6G) mobile networks are envisioned to extend ubiquitous intelligence beyond the terrestrial edge into space [1]. Aligned with this vision, space data centers 

(SpaceDCs), which are built on tight-formation satellite fleets with every satellite carrying a solar array and on-board GPUs, are emerging as a key platform for Space AI [2], [3]. Deploy-ment in a dawn–dusk sun-synchronous orbit (SSO) enables near-continuous and spatially uniform solar-energy harvesting across satellites, while inter-satellite distances of only hundreds of meters support high-bandwidth, low-latency connections among their GPUs [4], [5]. Together, these features make SpaceDCs a scalable, solar-powered computing platform for distributed 

large language models (LLMs) [6]. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is a widely adopted architecture for LLMs, comprising multiple expert networks that can be deployed across satellites [7]. Within this architecture, a gating mechanism activates experts according to the input token, and the activated experts perform

computation in parallel [8]. However, token-dependent expert activation creates heterogeneous power demands that mismatch the uniform solar-power supply across the SpaceDC. Under this 

supply–demand mismatch , satellites with higher power demand remain constrained by their local solar-power supply. This limits their computation speed and hence increases MoE inference latency. To tackle this challenge, we propose SpaceWPT, a system that explores wireless power transfer (WPT) in SpaceDCs to reallocate power among satellites to accelerate MoE inference. Existing methods mitigate the supply-demand mismatch by redistributing power demand across nodes through model placement at deployment or expert routing during inference. Model-placement strategies determine the power demand by assigning model components to computing nodes [9]. Several works optimize MoE expert placement according to token workloads and activation patterns to balance processing loads and reduce inter-node communication [10], [11]. However, inter-satellite link conditions in SpaceDCs vary continuously, causing the placement optimized at deployment to gradually become suboptimal. Adjusting the placement to the varying links requires transferring billion-parameter experts between satellites, incurring high communication overhead [12]. Alterna-tively, expert routing instead keeps model placement fixed and reshapes the token-level power demand by determining which experts process each token. Existing methods balance expert loads by routing tokens to less-loaded experts when gate scores are comparable [13], or reduce communication overhead by replacing or skipping experts based on representation similarity and channel conditions [14]. Nevertheless, such operations alter the MoE model output and hence degrade inference accuracy. The proposed WPT approach has no such limitations. Rather than balancing power demand through workload redistribution, it adjusts the supply side by reallocating harvested solar power among satellites. This leaves expert placement and routing unchanged, avoiding the overhead of transferring expert parameters and preserving inference accuracy. There exists a rich literature on the application of microwave-based WPT to power different types of terrestrial wireless networks, ranging from communication and sensor networks to edge computing networks. Relevant studies use downlink WPT to maximize uplink throughput [15] and deploy dedicated power beacons to optimize network deployment under outage constraints [16]. More recently, WPT has been integrated with edge computing and federated learning, where transferred energy powers local processing and wireless transmission to accelerate computation and communication at energy-constrained devices [17], [18], [19]. WPT in space operates under different scales of inter-node distances and power demands. Inter-satellite distances span hundreds of meters to kilometers, beyond the operating range of microwave-based WPT, while LLM inference requires substantially more power than low-power communication or sensing. These requirements favor laser-based WPT , whose low-divergence laser beams maintain high power density over long distances and whose narrow spectrum enables photovoltaic receivers to be optimized for high conversion efficiency [20], [21]. Moreover, operating in space avoids the safety constraints that limit high-power laser transmission in terrestrial envi-ronments [22]. Existing laser-based WPT studies investigate transferring surplus energy from large satellites to energy-deficient CubeSats, small satellites used for communication, and mitigating pointing errors to improve harvested power [23], [24]. These works establish the feasibility of laser-based WPT in space, but they mainly focus on point-to-point single-link design. However, WPT for distributed MoE in space is more sophisticated as it requires network-level coordinated designs across multiple satellites to accommodate their activation-based heterogeneous power demands. Designing network-level WPT for distributed MoE inference poses two unique challenges in terms of controlling transfer directions and power levels. First, transfer directions need to accommodate the uneven power demands induced by skewed expert activation. As expert activation varies from token to token, the locations of power shortages and surpluses change, requiring corresponding adjustments to inter-satellite WPT. Second, even with the transfer direction determined, the power level should be carefully controlled to balance the receiver’s power demand against the transmitter’s own computation needs. Increasing the transfer power accelerates receiver-side computation but leaves less power for computation at the transmitter, correspondingly increasing its latency. Furthermore, inter-satellite WPT incurs losses from electrical–optical con-version and beam spreading over the air, which complicates power control. Therefore, effective WPT control requires optimizing transfer directions and power levels according to the computation demands at both ends and the associated link efficiencies. To address these challenges, we present SpaceWPT, a WPT-assisted inference framework for low-latency token generation in SpaceDCs. SpaceWPT builds on the existing SpaceMoE architecture, where the attention and gating modules are deployed on a gateway satellite, while the expert modules are distributed across the remaining satellites [7]. Each token is processed by the attention module at the gateway and then by the activated experts in parallel, whose outputs are aggregated at the gateway. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to deploy WPT for LLM inference in space. The key contributions and findings of this work are summarized as follows.  

> •

Attention–Expert Latency Tradeoff: We theoretically characterize the effect of WPT power control on E2E MoE inference latency. To this end, two basic design principles are derived to guide the control of transfer directions and power levels. First, it is optimal for a satellite to transfer all its harvested power when its expert is inactive. Second, it never transmits and receives power simultaneously when its expert is active. Based on these principles, we construct a tractable surrogate for the inference latency under stochastic expert activation. The resulting surrogate reveals an attention–expert latency tradeoff: power transferred by an active expert to support attention computation reduces attention latency while increasing the expert latency. Balancing this tradeoff to reduce the total inference latency necessitates the optimization of power control in the sequel.  

> •

Activation-Based WPT Control: To balance the tradeoff between attention and expert latencies, we design a near-optimal strategy for WPT power controls by minimizing the latency surrogate. The resulting solution exhibits a two-sided water-filling structure. Compared with conventional water filling, which allocates power from a single source to multiple links, this structure allows each satellite to donate, retain, or receive power. In this structure, the water level is determined by expert activation statistics, the MoE inference workload, and WPT link efficiencies. Then, the transfer direction and power level of each satellite can be obtained by comparing its link efficiency with the water level. The solution shows that satellites with more efficient WPT links have higher power levels, while those with inefficient links tend to retain their harvested power. This strategy reduces the original high-dimensional problem to a single scalar equation for the water level while providing a provable near-optimality guarantee.  

> •

Experiments: Experiments with Mixtral-8x7B and Phi-3.5-MoE on HumanEval confirm the derived attention– expert tradeoff and the substantial latency reductions of SpaceWPT over No-WPT baselines. The latency gain increases as WPT efficiency improves and the SpaceDC formation becomes more compact, highlighting the benefit of efficient short-range WPT. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II presents the system model and performance metrics. Section III analyzes the inference latency and derives a tractable surrogate. Section IV develops the WPT power-control strategy, and Section V presents the experimental results. II. S YSTEM MODEL AND METRICS

E. System Operations 

We adopt expert parallelism for distributed MoE inference in the SpaceDC [33]. Each round begins with a gating decision at the gateway and ends before the subsequent gating decision. The operations within one round are illustrated in Fig. 3(b) and described as follows. 1) Gating: Given the attention output from the preceding round, the gateway performs the gating operation de-scribed in Section II-A, yielding the activation state s

and the active expert set A(s) defined in Section II-A1. 2) WPT Power Control: Based on s, the gateway config-ures the WPT transmit powers using the power-control solution derived in Section IV, over the star topology described in Section II-D2. 3) Activation Transmission: The gateway transmits the input activation to the active experts over the FSO data links modeled in Section II-C. 4) Expert Computation: Each active expert processes the received activation using its onboard FFN, following the MoE inference model in Section II-A. 5) Token Aggregation: Each active expert returns its FFN output to the gateway over the FSO data link. The gateway aggregates the received outputs to obtain the layer output. 6) Attention Computation: The gateway computes the atten-tion output from the aggregated expert output and passes it to the gating module, thereby initiating the next round. During Steps 3–5, the active-expert paths operate in parallel, where each expert starts its FFN computation upon receiving the activation and returns its output upon completion. B. SpaceWPT Performance 

At the default operating point, Fig. 7 shows that the analytical controls reduce the E2E latency of Mixtral in two stages. No-WPT incurs 5.7041 ms/token, whereas Uni-WPT reduces the latency to 5.2319 ms/token, representing an 8.28% 

improvement. SpaceWPT further reduces it to 4.6238 ms/token, which is 11 .62% below Uni-WPT and 18 .94% below No-WPT. The Uni-WPT gain comes entirely from optimal idle-expert donation. As characterized in Lemma 1, each idle expert transmits its full solar-power budget, delivering 285 .62 W on average to the gateway after WPT loss and raising its compute power from 69 .09 to 354 .71 W, as shown in Fig. 7(a). At the default operating point, all active experts remain neutral, with 

P ⋆k = 0 , so their computation latency is unchanged from No-WPT. The resulting 8.28% reduction therefore stems solely from the shorter gateway attention latency. SpaceWPT further reduces the latency by reallocating part of the gateway power to the active experts. Compared with Uni-WPT, the gateway allocates an average of 138 .18 W to active-expert WPT and retains 216 .53 W for attention computation, as shown in Fig. 7(a). This reallocation increases the attention-latency component by 0.1165 ms/token but reduces the critical-expert component by 0.7246 ms/token, yielding the net E2E latency reduction of 11 .62% in Fig. 7(b).

VI. C ONCLUDING REMARKS 

This work advocates inter-satellite WPT to support low-latency distributed inference in SpaceDCs. The key feature lies in introducing a new degree of freedom for space computing, i.e., WPT-enabled energy migration, to cope with imbalanced computing loads. Such a power-sharing policy redistributes energy according to demand under spatially uniform sunlight exposure. The developed principles for migration direction and activation-based WPT control provide theoretical guidance for the development of emerging SpaceDCs in dawn–dusk SSOs. This work opens a new frontier for integrating WPT into distributed computing in space. One direction is to extend the WPT design to more general topologies, where direct power exchange between expert satellites allows more flexible 12 

power redistribution according to computation demands and link efficiencies. Another direction is to unify the separate laser-based WPT and communication links into a single optical link that supports both energy delivery and data transmission. Such integration could reduce onboard hardware requirements while calling for joint designs that balance WPT efficiency and communication performance. VII. A PPENDIX
